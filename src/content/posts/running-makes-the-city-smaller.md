I want to be able to say, “I'll be there in thirty,” and cross half the city on foot.

Phone, keys, wallet. Start moving. The distance is something I can cover myself,
without arranging a ride or deciding where a bicycle will spend the next few hours.

That is one of the things I am rebuilding as I return to training. I want good times
on the track, across the range from 800 to 5000 metres. I also want the freedom that
used to come with being a runner: places becoming close enough that I can simply go.

## Getting home

There is a run in my old records that captures this better than a race result.

July 6, 2021. Four seventeen in the morning. Sofia. I had been out with friends and
ran home: [7.59 kilometres in 34:04 of moving time](https://www.strava.com/activities/5581962035),
at about 4:29 per kilometre.

I was living away from home for a research programme that summer. The run took me
across the city from Studentski, along empty boulevards. I remember it as a lot of
fun. Getting home on foot was a possibility so ordinary that I could choose it at
the end of a night out.

The pace tells me something about my old fitness. The decision tells me what
that fitness made possible. Seven or eight kilometres did not require a separate
occasion. I could run them because I needed to get somewhere.

That changes the scale of a city. A place several kilometres away becomes a place
I can reach in half an hour outside. The distance is still there, but it stops
being a reason to arrange transport.

I had been remembering my old training mainly through the workouts: the fast
200s, the repeated 900s, the time trials. Then this memory brought back the rest
of it. Running also happened between the workouts, inside an ordinary day.

## A city I can move through

Thinking about San Francisco this way makes me smile. I look at the city and
imagine a much larger set of places I can reach casually: another neighbourhood,
a park, the ocean. A trip can begin because I want to see somewhere, with the run
itself part of the reason to go.

I am already bringing small pieces of that back. On October 2, I ran to the gym
in Nob Hill and back. Later I ran to Mission to get a burrito and returned. These
were useful journeys, and running fit inside them.

The longer outings are still ahead. An hour of comfortable running used to fit
inside my life. Recovering that ease, with a pace around 4:30 per kilometre
feeling enjoyable and familiar again, would bring much more of the city within
reach. I cannot assume that pace today.

For now, the habit can begin with ten or fifteen minutes around the block. Then
fifteen or twenty-five minutes somewhere a little farther away. I can become
accustomed to the activity before deciding that every free evening needs to
produce an impressive route.

## The track and the city

The track has a different job. There I want to be precise: known distances,
recorded repetitions, consistent recovery, and an honest account of how the
effort felt. Repeating a workout gives me something to compare. Changing it needs
a reason.

City running gives me room to play. I can choose a destination, explore a
neighbourhood, or fit a run around something I already need to do. I do not want
to carry an interval session's negotiation with the stopwatch into every trip
outside.

During the [8 October track session](/running/record/2026/#8-october--lt-31200-m--1-min),
I could feel my stride opening again and the running felt relaxed. The city runs
can grow around that returning ease. Prescribing my old cruising pace now would
turn every outing into another test.

The load is where these two kinds of running meet. I am already increasing the
speed of the track work. More repetitions and longer runs would add other demands.
When we started discussing all of those increases together, I called it an injury
landmine. Enjoying an outing does not make its physical cost disappear. The
freedom I want has to be something I can keep using.

## What has to come with me

There is an adult complication that was much less important in that Sofia
memory: the laptop. As a kid, I could simply run. Now I often need to move the
things that let me work.

Much of my life now involves a computer in a backpack. I might want to work from
a different place, and the machine needs to arrive with me. A bicycle handles
that cargo well. It also brings decisions about locking it, storing it, and
where I will leave it when I move on. Those decisions matter precisely because
the simplicity of running is part of its appeal.

I would like to carry a phone, payment card, ID and a key or two, and still
resume work when I arrived somewhere suitable.

That means solving a problem beyond fitness. I would need a screen, a keyboard
or another usable way to give instructions, and access to my computing
environment. The example I have in mind is the computer cluster at Stanford:
arrive somewhere equipped to work, instead of carrying the equipment everywhere.
I have not solved the equivalent arrangement for everyday life here.

For now, the laptop gives different journeys different requirements. Some trips
can be light enough to run. Others need the things in the backpack. Becoming fit
enough to cross town will not, by itself, remove that distinction.

I want leaving home to require a decision about where to go, and very little else.
