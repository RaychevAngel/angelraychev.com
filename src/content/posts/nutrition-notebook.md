I want food to support training, recovery and health while remaining enjoyable to eat. The practical beginning is a hotel room, a daily stop at Trader Joe's, and roast chicken from RT. Storage and appetite are real constraints. A diet assembled to fill every cell in a nutrient table can still fail as a meal.

This notebook holds the current pattern and the decisions behind it. The [food record](/nutrition/record/) preserves what happened, including portions I did not measure and food I could not finish. The [nutrient guide](/nutrition/guide/) gives each nutrient its own explanation, with the applicable intake references, the current estimate and the evidence behind a judgment. The [investigations](/nutrition/research/) connect questions that span several nutrients or concern a food, supplement or experimental intervention.

## What the first attempts taught me

The oversized bowl contained rice, chicken, beans, sardines and olives. I had already eaten fruit, yogurt, nuts and vegetables. The bowl was too much, its combinations were unappealing, and much of it went to waste. That is a meaningful result: a proposed eating pattern failed on volume, taste and storage before its micronutrient totals could make it useful.

I enjoyed the yogurt, almonds, raspberries, bananas, orange, apple, tomato, avocado and peppers. A better arrangement was becoming clear: eat the fruit, yogurt and nuts during the day, then have a simple savory meal. Beans and olives were removed. A different small fish was tried, but the anchovies were intensely salty. The original sardines remained a tolerable option rather than a favorite.

The next pattern felt better. I liked the food and reported slight hunger before bed. That observation helps judge whether a day was comfortable; it does not identify maintenance calories. Food quantities, training demands and body-weight trends would have to be documented repeatedly before assigning an energy target.

## The current foundation

The latest reported day contains nonfat plain European-style yogurt, walnuts and almonds, four bananas, raspberries, an orange, a tomato, avocado, one red pepper, a basmati rice pouch, half a roast chicken, chimichurri and the original skinless, boneless sardines. Vitamin D and broth were forgotten that day. Planned purchases and supplements have their own status; they enter intake totals only when actually consumed.

Some quantities are clear: the entire 32 oz yogurt tub, four bananas and one rice pouch were confirmed. Others remain estimates: the nuts were described by count, a half chicken has an uncertain edible weight, and the sauce’s oil content is unverified. The nonfat yogurt’s nutrient label is still provisional. The eating date is also unconfirmed because the report arrived after midnight. These limitations stay attached to the [record](/nutrition/record/) rather than disappearing inside a precise-looking total.

This foundation is adjustable. Walnuts are a food I like, and fruit substitutions such as kiwi, mango or melon are worth trying for enjoyment and variety. Optional additions should earn their place through a useful contribution, good taste, manageable volume and realistic storage. An interesting study is a reason to investigate a food, not an obligation to buy another item every day.

## What I would change next

Keep the arrangement that worked: yogurt, fruit and nuts during the day, with a simple savory meal. Try a different fruit in place of some bananas or the apple; keep walnuts because I enjoy them. The [fruit](/nutrition/research/fruit-substitutions/) and [nut](/nutrition/research/nut-choices/) investigations explain the nutritional tradeoffs without requiring a larger meal.

Follow through on the vitamin D purchase I had planned, and choose a manageable vitamin A food substitution from the [vitamin D](/nutrition/guide/vitamin-d/) and [vitamin A](/nutrition/guide/vitamin-a/) guides. These remain decisions to try; a planned supplement or uneaten food never enters the daily totals.

For fish, compare the label for the whole portion and prioritize a preparation I like. The intensely salty anchovies need not become a daily meal. [Small fish and other seafood](/nutrition/research/fish-seafood/) can be tried as a simple meal or a rotation with chicken, keeping appetite and storage in view.

The highest-value information check is the actual nonfat yogurt label, followed by the chicken's edible amount and the sauce portion. These inputs affect several estimates at once. [Iodine](/nutrition/guide/iodine/) and restaurant [sodium](/nutrition/guide/sodium/) remain important unknowns; a missing database value is no reason to supplement or add salt.

I would leave further optimization optional until storage and the ordinary routine are stable. The [training and recovery research](/nutrition/research/#recovery) can guide a specific experiment when there is a question to answer. It does not make broth, powders or an experimental compound necessary purchases.

## How I want to make changes

First, make the pattern work as food. A useful change can be a substitution, a smaller portion, better timing or a more coherent meal. It need not increase the shopping list. The practical questions are whether I enjoy it, can finish it, can store it, and feel able to train on it.

Second, distinguish a dietary estimate from body status. A low daily estimate can prompt review without establishing a clinical deficiency. Missing iodine data, for example, means the saved food sources cannot quantify intake; it does not mean I ate no iodine. The guide gives the uncertainty a visible place.

Third, use the right reference for the question. Recommended intakes, adequate intakes, upper limits and reduction thresholds have different purposes. The food-label Daily Value is another reference again. The guide retains those distinctions and the nutrient forms to which a limit applies. [NIH dietary reference definitions](https://ods.od.nih.gov/HealthInformation/nutrientrecommendations.aspx).

Finally, connect a change to an outcome. A nutrient's physiological role does not establish that additional intake improves performance. A food can fit the diet without treating an injury. A supplement can alter a blood marker without demonstrating a health benefit. The evidence belongs beside the specific claim it supports.

## Training and recovery

I am returning to heavier training, with running and resistance work. The [running notebook](/running-notebook/) holds the programme and progression; the food record can retain what I ate, appetite and training context without pretending to measure recovery from a meal.

The research connects [protein and available energy](/nutrition/research/protein-energy-and-training/), [bone nutrition and loading](/nutrition/research/calcium-vitamin-d-and-bone-loading/), muscle adaptation and connective-tissue questions. [Broth and collagen](/nutrition/research/collagen-broth-and-connective-tissue/) are of interest because I prefer drinking broth to taking collagen powder. The useful questions include what a product actually contains and whether the human evidence addresses outcomes relevant to my training.

## A record that can change

Ordinary days should be easy to add. Each entry retains the reported foods, portions, actually taken supplements, estimated nutrients and the observations available that day. An estimate remains an estimate after it enters a table. A correction explains what changed and keeps the earlier observation intelligible.

The current judgments can change as product labels, portions, repeated eating records and research improve. Historical entries describe what was known at the time. The notebook holds the current interpretation, while the supporting pages leave enough detail to understand how it was reached.
