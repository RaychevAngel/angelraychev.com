My larger project is human agency: the ability to act, and to keep acting
throughout life. In [The Optimization Nobody Is Running](/the-optimization-nobody-is-running/),
I describe capability as something whose level matters throughout that life.
Running is one concrete way to work on the physical part of the ambition.

I want to build elite physical capability and preserve it for life. Running is
my foundation, from 100 m to the marathon, with strength and other physical
skills alongside it. I think about the body through an engine, a structure and
a driver: supplying the work, carrying it and coordinating the movement.
Running appeals to me because it is such a simple, natural way to put those
capacities to work. Its full range asks for speed as well as endurance. Strength
and other skills extend what I can do beyond the abilities a running time tests.

Health and a longer functional life are part of what I hope
that capability will support; event performances alone cannot establish those
outcomes. Staying able to train, without repeatedly losing continuity to injury,
is already part of the project.

For now, I am concentrating on **800+ points on the World Athletics scoring
tables across the whole 800–5000 m range**: 800, 1000, 1500, mile, 2000, 3000,
two miles and 5000. I want that performance profile in one runner. The common
scale makes my strengths and gaps visible; the training has to turn the
ambition into something I can actually do.

## Where I am starting

Two kilometres has a particular place in my running. It was a childhood event
distance and the length of a neighbourhood loop on which I accumulated a great
deal of running. I remember **8:33 on that loop in sixth grade**. Separate Kids
Run results were **8:31 at age 12** and **8:13 at age 14**. I no longer live near
that loop, but it remains a personal anchor. Two kilometres also sits at the
geometric midpoint of 800 and 5000: 800:2000 = 2000:5000. That is pleasing;
it does not make the distance a uniquely revealing physiological test.

By 2022 I was racing on the track, with **2:03.62 for 800 m** and **4:23.90 for
1500 m** at the June national championships. That season also included repeated
injury interruptions. The [2022 account](/running/record/2022/) follows the
workouts, pacing decisions and races through the
[July U20 national championships](/running/record/2022/#9-july--u20-nationals-1500-m),
beyond the June peak. It gives me both a level to return toward and a reason to
take continuity seriously.

The years since were uneven. I ran intermittently in 2023 and 2024, including
substantial long runs, interval work in early 2024 and the
[Orange County Marathon on 5 May 2024](/running/record/2023-2025/#5-may--orange-county-marathon).
The large gap was 2025: my Strava has one run recorded for the year. Earlier
2026 self-timed 5Ks were around 20:30. What I am rebuilding now is regular,
repeatable middle-distance training, with some experience still available and
plenty of fitness to recover.

The first event benchmark of this return was **7:16.67 for 2000 m on 27 September**. It is a
**181-point table reference**, beside the **855-point 5:30 goal**. The trial was
self-timed, with its method and pacing retained in the record. After it, I
wrote, “At least we are on the scale.” The gap is real, and I now have a starting
point from which later training and tests can mean something.

The [11 October 5000 m trial](/running/record/2026/#11-october--5000-m-time-trial)
now adds a continuous longer-distance reference: **19:59 reported, 3:59.8/km,
161 points**, beside the **810-point 15:00 goal**. The phone failed near the
finish; the dated account keeps that estimate with the splits. These two
different events locate the current return within the larger project.

My [personal bests and recorded best efforts](/running/record/results/#personal-bests)
have one table of original times, paces, World Athletics points, dates and race
or training context, from 300 m to the marathon. It shows a stronger short end.
The marks come from different years and settings, so they do not form a
simultaneous fitness test. Nonstandard distances retain their original marks
beside a labelled same-pace comparison in a named discipline; the mile
reference scales the documented 1600 m pace to the full mile.

## The runner I am trying to build

I began with an **equal 810-point curve** from an IAAF calculator: 1:58.03 for
800 m, 4:03.85 for 1500 m and 15:00.02 for 5000 m were examples of the same
score at different distances. The tables are now published by World Athletics.
That common scale is the backbone of my comparisons: where does my performance
fall away as the event gets longer, and is the whole curve rising?

I later chose round goal times that put the strongest part of the intended
profile around 800–2000 m. The central goals span **810–862 points**, with
800+ as the shared floor. The scores below use the men's outdoor columns of
the [2025 revised World Athletics tables](https://worldathletics.org/download/download?filename=4f77dcb3-2945-4c58-ad8b-955a999b13e8.pdf&urlslug=World+Athletics+Scoring+Tables+of+Athletics):

For orientation, exactly **800 points** corresponds to **1:58.43 for 800 m**,
**4:04.73 for 1500 m** and **15:03.36 for 5000 m** in this edition. The rounded
goals sit above that floor by different amounts. The official lookup awards
the lower score when a mark falls between two listed thresholds; a calculator
that interpolates or rounds can differ by a point.

The wider picture includes **12.00 for 100 m (615 points), 24.00 for 200 m
(671), 52.00 for 400 m (744), 1:24 for 600 m (798), and 33:00 for 10,000 m
(717)**. The lower scores at the edges define the shape I want: strongest in the
middle distances, with speed below them and useful endurance above them. They
do not turn the present programme into simultaneous preparation for every
distance. These are ambitions, with no completion date attached.

When I discussed the profile, I kept returning to Jakob Ingebrigtsen and Josh
Kerr as references for its shape and for how high the ceiling could be. I was
imagining my own curve at a much lower point level, with particular interest
in 1500 m and the mile. That comparison keeps the ambition open; it does not
give me their training requirements or establish that my eventual curve will
have the same shape.

Getting the whole range above 800 points means closing the gap toward the
longer events as well as improving the 800. A promising interval session helps
me choose the next workout. An event performance is what moves the scored
curve.

## Why the programme changed

I initially imagined a long tempo-first build. I then chose to start with a
small mixture of sustainable work (**LT**), work intended to approach maximal
oxygen uptake (**VO₂**) and fast running (**F**), with easy days between them.
That let me observe more than one part of the returning runner without starting
with large sessions.

There was another change of mind. At first, I liked varying workouts: it could
keep every week from becoming a judgment against last week's stopwatch. Once I
had a few actual sessions, I wanted to see what would happen if I repeated them.
If the same 3×1200 was becoming quicker and more manageable, why replace it
with something that changed both the distance and the comparison?

Some of the work already feels familiar. The 900s bring back memories, and I
described the three-minute efforts of 3×800 as feeling like home. Recognisable
work makes pace, effort and reserve easier to compare. I can improve within a
workout, then earn a change of structure, instead of changing everything at
once.

That is also a durability question. I want regular track work and the freedom
to run around the city; adding both quickly could bring back the interruptions
I am trying to leave behind. A programme is useful only if I can keep doing it.
Faster repetitions, more volume and more ordinary movement all have to fit into
the same life and the same body.

The experimental work changed too. A fixed anaerobic-capacity **A** slot became
**X**, a place to try a candidate session. My October 6 attempt at 10 seconds
hard / 10 seconds recovery was broken into two sets by a phone failure. Its
approximate distance included recovery movement, so turning that distance into
a performance score would have rewarded the wrong thing. An experiment can
still help me decide whether a format belongs in the recurring programme; it
need not show a training improvement each time.

## A programme that earns its next step

The present plan uses **18 days**: six blocks of two training days followed by a
chill day. Across the cycle there are four LT sessions, four VO₂ sessions, one
stable F session, one mixed-rest **FA**, one event time trial and one **X**
experiment. The [programme page](/running/record/2026/programme/) keeps the exact
order, templates and progression tables.

The cycle began on **15 October**. Its eleven specified workouts stay committed
through this cycle; **X** remains flexible, and structural changes wait for the
next cycle's review. I have not yet completed a full cycle and shown that I can
sustain it. Its cost also extends beyond the listed workouts:
running to the gym, carrying a backpack and a hard bike commute still count as
movement, even on a day whose programme says chill.

LT and VO₂ advance independently. The first LT gate was **3×1200 m averaging
4:48, with another repetition plausibly available**. Passing it on October 8
unlocked **3×1600 m**. The longer workout's eventual **6:08** gate is a later
target, rather than a compulsory pace on the first attempt.

On **15 October**, VO₂ independently passed its **3×800 m, 2:40 average** gate,
without a major final collapse. That earns **3×900 m** next. Its **2:55.5 average**
is the next graduation target, rather than a compulsory entry pace. Recovery
remains nominally **5:30**; feeling ready earlier is not an automatic reason to
shorten it. The point is to give each repetition a chance to produce the intended
work.

The later rows describe ambitious directions, not completed fitness or proof
that the exact steps are optimal. Keeping the workout stable long enough to
understand it makes a change of structure meaningful.

## What the observations now tell me

Both interval progressions have now earned longer repetitions. On October 8,
I ran three 1200 m repetitions with one minute between them:
**4:42.5, 4:42.7, 4:41.3**. They
averaged **4:42.17 (3:55.1/km)**, with a 1.4-second spread. I finished feeling
that a fourth repetition was definitely available, possibly a fifth. September
19's same nominal workout had averaged **4:56.33 (4:06.9/km)**, and I had been
spent after the third. The later session passed the 4:48 gate in both time and
intended character.

Before the September session, a hard hilly bike ride had already left my legs
burning. The improvement is an observed change in performance and feel;
attributing all 14 seconds per repetition to training adaptation would ignore
that starting condition. The intervening closed-track session used a longer
asphalt loop with elevation changes, so its times cannot be dropped into the
same comparison as measured track 1200s.

On the VO₂ side, 3×800 with the same 5:30 recovery moved from **2:48 average
(3:30/km) on September 22** to **2:44 (3:25/km) on October 1**. The later session
felt more limited by breathing and less by the legs. That change in experience
is useful, although it does not measure oxygen uptake or identify the cause of
the improvement.

On [October 9](/running/record/2026/#9-october--vo-3800-m-with-interrupted-timing),
I completed **~2:43, 2:39.4, 2:39.3**. The first is a conservative estimate
after the phone died at the finish. Another shutdown added a partial 250–300 m
effort and a 60–90-second walk before restarting the second full 800. All three
full repetitions were done, and the last two were tightly matched despite a
very hard finish without a sprint. Using the first estimate gives about
**2:40.6 (3:20.7/km)** on average; it may have been quicker. This was a good
completed session. I left wanting another 3×800 to cement the 2:40 gate with
reliable timing.

The fast work needs a different comparison. September 24's six 200s averaged
33.64, relaxed and closely grouped. On October 5, after an overly relaxed first
repetition, the last five averaged 31.82. I was making more deliberate effort to
maintain knee lift, quick return and posture within each repetition. The effort
changed along with the times. Those faster 200s tell me something about fast
running; an 800 time trial still has to answer the endurance question.

On [October 12](/running/record/2026/#12-october--f-6200-m--3-min), the same
6×200 m / 3 min structure averaged **30.76**. The first four averaged **31.25**;
I then pushed the last two to be more sprinty, running **30.22 and 29.30**.
It felt good. One repetition is now below thirty seconds in this series,
with the extra intent recorded beside it. I want to see how repeatable that
speed becomes with the movement I am trying to preserve.

After October 8's LT session, I thought the earlier fast work had helped make it more relaxed,
even while I was still sore from F and A. My fast stride was opening and my
knees were coming higher; slower running around 4:00–4:05/km seemed to need very
little push. Whether the returning movement has lowered the cost of that
running, and which work helped it return, are questions to investigate.

The [11 October 5000 m](/running/record/2026/#11-october--5000-m-time-trial)
made the continuous-running question concrete. I report **19:59 (3:59.8/km),
worth 161 points**. The first four kilometres were **3:58, 4:00, 4:02, 4:02**,
reaching 4000 m in **16:02**; the final **3:57** is estimated from the accepted
finish after the phone shut down in the last 50 m. This was one of the most
unpleasant efforts of my life. My legs were heavy, breathing felt spent, and
posture and knee lift faded after about 2500–3000 m. I broadly maintained the
pace, but could no longer maintain the way I wanted to run.

I later clarified that I brought the posture and knee lift back together over
the final two laps, especially the last. Holding good movement through the
difficult middle and finding it again for the finish are different observations.

The controlled LT intervals had been about **3:55/km**; this continuous effort
was about **4:00/km**, with a much greater felt cost. The trial adds the duration
and continuity that the repetitions could not establish. The longer LT structure
was already earned; VO₂ still needed its own confirmation. The movement I can
maintain belongs beside the time when I compare efforts.

That confirmation came on
[15 October](/running/record/2026/#15-october--vo-3800-m--530):
**2:35.26, 2:35.11, 2:36.27**, averaging **2:35.55 (3:14.4/km)** with a
**1.16-second spread**. The nominal recovery was 5:30; COROS recorded **5:25.67
and 5:20.82** between the efforts. The three-repetition average is 3.80 seconds
quicker than October 9's last two, although that earlier session's interruptions
make it an imperfect comparison.

It was extremely hard, but easier than October 9: “hard, not disgusting.”
Breathing felt maximal in the last 200 m, yet I stayed composed, felt a kick
was available and deliberately did not sprint. Finishing naturally felt like
home. A brief loss of coordination early in the third repetition settled before
the next curve. The gate is passed through the whole set and the way I completed
it; the next question is how that control carries into 900s.

The feverish feeling had gone before bed on 14 October; my left Achilles was
quiet during and after the workout.

The first [3×1600 m on 16 October](/running/record/2026/#16-october--lt-31600-m--100-m-walks)

The nearly even times concealed how much harder the effort felt. Heat
complicates the fitness comparison; it does not supply a numerical correction.
The session makes preserving good movement through the middle of a hard

On [19 October](/running/record/2026/#19-october--vo-3900-m--nominal-530), the first

The next aim is more even execution and a lower average at the same format.

On [21 October](/running/record/2026/#21-october--fa-4200-m--3-min--4200-m--90-seconds),

The opening repetitions felt controlled, about **0.94 seconds quicker** than

I think holding that speed was impressive, and I want to repeat the format.
The nearly even times also conceal the late breakdown. It is a concrete

The [second 3×1600 m on 23 October](/running/record/2026/#23-october--lt-31600-m--nominal-1-min)

## Scores and rarity

The score gives me an equivalent performance at another event. The same point
total at 800 and 5000 may still require very different work from me. My ambition
is to bring both ends, and the events between them, into the same performance
band.

Rarity needs a population as well as a score. The
[official tables](https://worldathletics.org/download/download?filename=4f77dcb3-2945-4c58-ad8b-955a999b13e8.pdf&urlslug=World+Athletics+Scoring+Tables+of+Athletics)
use athletics statistics to compare performances, but they do not provide a
percentile among all men or all runners. An annual best-by-athlete list can
place a mark among recorded competitors; a race's results can place it among
that race's finishers. Neither denominator describes everybody who could run
the time.

One concrete reference is World Athletics' **2025 men's senior 800 m list**,
checked in October 2026 with “Best by Athlete,” world-wide coverage, electronic
timing and both outdoor and indoor performances. It lists **7241 athletes
strictly faster than my 1:56 goal**, and **10,973 strictly faster than 1:58.43**,
the outdoor 800-point threshold. These are counts against times in that
published list, whose reporting limit is 2:00.00; they are not a percentile
among all runners or a count of equal-scoring indoor and outdoor results. The
tied boundary rows are on [page 73](https://worldathletics.org/records/toplists/middlelong/800-metres/all/men/senior/2025?ageCategory=senior&bestResultsOnly=true&eventId=10229501&maxResultsByCountry=all&page=73&regionType=world&timing=electronic)
and [page 110](https://worldathletics.org/records/toplists/middlelong/800-metres/all/men/senior/2025?ageCategory=senior&bestResultsOnly=true&eventId=10229501&maxResultsByCountry=all&page=110&regionType=world&timing=electronic).

Rarity-based scoring systems do exist. A
[2007 paper in the IAAF's research journal](https://worldathletics.org/download/downloadnsa?filename=47a3d069-d468-4d0b-994f-a9dabacda43d.pdf&urlslug=the-physical-basis-of-scoring-the-athletic-pe)
discusses Dale Harder's separate scale, in which a further 100 points represents
a tenfold reduction in the qualifying fraction. That rule belongs to a
different scoring system; applying it directly to my World Athletics points
would invent a conversion the current tables do not establish.

The rarity of the entire 800–5000 profile would require linked performances
from the same athletes across the range. The events are related, so multiplying
separate event rarities would not answer that question. An athlete who has no
listed result at one distance may simply never have raced it.

## The working model

The programme's labels describe intended kinds of work. They give me a way to
ask what a session is for before judging it by how hard it felt.

**F — fast running.** Produce a useful fast velocity with good movement and enough
recovery to repeat it. How fast I can run and how long I can preserve that
movement are separate questions. The current benchmark is 6×200 m with three
minutes between repetitions; it is not an all-out 200 m test.

**VO₂ — work near maximal oxygen uptake.** The quantity I want is actual time
near that state. Minutes at an externally chosen pace do not directly measure
it. How quickly oxygen uptake rises, how long the effort remains tolerable and
what recovery restores all enter the question.

**LT — sustainable high lactate turnover.** I am interested in what can be
sustained while lactate appearance and disposal remain balanced at a high rate.
Blood concentration alone cannot tell me those rates. LT is also a working label
for my hard, controlled repetitions in support of 3000–5000 m. I have not
established their relation to maximal lactate steady state or critical speed.

**A — anaerobic capacity.** I am exploring the idea that time spent with a
finite reservoir deeply depleted might matter, rather than merely recording
the highest instantaneous output.

My present measurements are field times, felt effort and event results; I have
no direct measurements of oxygen uptake, lactate flux or anaerobic capacity.
The LT and VO₂ labels name the intended work, rather than certify a measured
internal state. Faster LT-labelled running might reflect changed lactate
handling, improved movement, different pacing, better recovery or several
changes together.

The fuller argument belongs in [What should a running workout maximize?](/what-should-a-running-workout-maximize/).
Here, the model has to produce decisions that I can carry onto the track and
observations that can inform the next decision.
