## The comparison that prompted this

I was surprised that the potassium estimate was much higher than sodium. That can happen with a diet containing substantial yogurt, fruit and vegetables. **Potassium exceeding sodium in milligrams is not, by itself, a problem.** The two nutrients have different intake references; the aim is not to make their totals equal.

There is also a missing-data problem. The saved estimate reviewed on 10 October 2026 contains approximately **7,017 mg potassium from 12 of 13 foods**, and **753 mg sodium from 11 of 13 foods**. Sodium in the RT chicken and chimichurri is unquantified. Potassium in the sauce is unquantified too. Those figures are available-value subtotals, not measured full-day intakes. The narrow numerical scenarios shown by the calculator cannot bound missing restaurant salt or every portion assumption.

Consequently, removing the fish would not establish that my full day contained less than 1,000 mg sodium. It would remove the fish's known contribution while leaving restaurant sodium unknown.

## What the references mean here

For a man aged 19–30, the US potassium **Adequate Intake is 3,400 mg/day**. Sodium's AI is **1,500 mg/day**, with a **Chronic Disease Risk Reduction recommendation to reduce usual intake above 2,300 mg/day**. These are different reference types, not a required sodium-to-potassium balance. An AI is not a diagnostic minimum, and the sodium reduction threshold is not a target to approach. The current National Academies framework sets no UL for either nutrient; an absent UL does not mean unlimited intake is safe. [National Academies, 2019, summary tables S-1 and S-2](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25353/chapter/2).

The **4,700 mg potassium Daily Value** used on food labels is a separate labeling reference. It should not silently replace my age- and sex-specific AI. [FDA Daily Values](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/daily-value-nutrition-and-supplement-facts-labels).

The [potassium entry](/nutrition/guide/potassium/) distinguishes food intake from blood potassium. No blood-pressure, electrolyte or kidney measurements are reported in the present log.

## A food pattern can matter beyond its sodium number

The original DASH feeding trial enrolled **459 adults**, then compared diets for eight weeks while keeping sodium and body weight constant. The pattern combining fruit, vegetables, low-fat dairy and lower saturated and total fat reduced systolic/diastolic pressure by **5.5/3.0 mmHg more than the control diet**. The fruit-and-vegetable arm lowered systolic pressure, but its diastolic contrast was not statistically significant. This supports a dietary pattern; it does not identify potassium as the sole active ingredient. [Appel et al., 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9099655/).

DASH-Sodium separately randomized **412 participants** to DASH or a control diet, with three sodium levels in random order for 30 days each. At 2,100 kcal, sodium targets were approximately **3,450, 2,300 and 1,150 mg/day**, scaled with energy requirements. Reducing sodium lowered pressure within both diets. Combining DASH and low sodium produced a larger reduction than high-sodium control. These were short-term blood-pressure outcomes, rather than stroke outcomes or a prediction of my response. The lowest tested target was not a universal prescription for every participant. [Sacks et al., 2001](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJM200101043440101).

My yogurt-and-produce pattern overlaps with some DASH foods. It is not a verified reproduction of the trial diet, and its blood-pressure effect cannot be inferred from the overlap.

## More potassium does not guarantee more benefit

In a **40-person crossover trial** of people at moderate cardiovascular risk, 64 mmol potassium chloride daily for six weeks lowered brachial systolic pressure compared with placebo: **131.8 versus 137.1 mmHg**. However, endothelial-function measures and pulse-wave analysis did not improve. A favorable blood-pressure result was not an improvement in every vascular endpoint. [Graham et al., 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/jhh201389).

An older trial offers a useful counterexample. Its main analysis followed **287 hypertensive men aged 45–68** after supervised medication withdrawal. Both groups reduced sodium; one received 96 mmol potassium chloride daily. Over an average **2.2 years**, 79 men in each group required medication again, with no significant blood-pressure difference. This does not negate all potassium benefits. It shows that adding potassium did not deliver the hoped-for medication benefit under those study conditions. [Grimm et al., 1990](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJM199003013220901).

These are supplement trials in selected populations, not evidence that I should add potassium to an already potassium-rich estimated food day. Their doses describe the research.

## Salt substitutes have outcome evidence, with important boundaries

SSaSS randomized **600 rural Chinese villages**, including **20,995 people with prior stroke or older age and high blood pressure**, to regular salt or a substitute containing **75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride**. Over a mean **4.74 years**, stroke rates were **29.14 versus 33.65 events per 1,000 person-years**, favoring substitution; rate ratio **0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.96**. Both sodium and potassium exposure changed, so this cannot isolate a potassium-only effect.

Serious events attributed to hyperkalemia did not differ significantly. That safety outcome was clinical, not routine screening for every biochemical potassium elevation. The trial excluded households with known serious kidney disease, potassium-sparing diuretic use or potassium supplementation. These limits matter when applying the result outside the trial population. [Neal et al., 2021](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675).

Impaired kidney excretion and medicines such as ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone or amiloride can make potassium intake consequential even below the AI. A potassium-containing salt substitute or supplement requires particular care in those settings. I have not supplied the kidney or medication context needed to personalize that decision. [NIH medication interactions](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Potassium-HealthProfessional/).

## What changes in my daily food plan

The first useful improvement is to verify the yogurt label and obtain better information about salt in the chicken, sauce and ayran. I can choose a fish I enjoy with a less salty label without using its sodium to fill an assumed deficit. If I replace bananas with kiwi, mango or another fruit, taste, appetite and a workable daily purchase are sufficient reasons; the high potassium subtotal alone does not establish a need to remove bananas.

Short track sessions and lifting do not quantify my sweat losses. The contemplated sauna has not been confirmed as part of the routine. Electrolyte replacement should respond to the actual session and losses, rather than the ratio between two food subtotals. The broader food pattern, repeatable intake information and measured blood pressure would answer different parts of this question. For the current hotel plan, potassium supplementation and an ideal-ratio target have no established personal need.
