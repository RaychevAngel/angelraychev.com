## CALERIE studied a sustained intervention with close support

CALERIE randomized **220 healthy, nonobese adults; 218 started**. Prescribed restriction was 25%; the achieved two-year average was **11.7%**, with approximately **10% weight loss**. Participants received intensive counseling and monitoring. Primary outcomes were body-composition-adjusted resting metabolism and core temperature. The metabolic difference appeared at twelve months but was null at twenty-four; temperature did not differ. Eight restriction participants temporarily stopped for safety concerns, including bone-density loss and treatment-resistant anemia; three permanently stopped for unresolved findings. NIH funds supported it; NIA staff contributed to interpretation and manuscript preparation. [Ravussin and colleagues, original report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4841173/)

This supported intervention did not measure a lifespan benefit. The [current trial record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00427193) lists the same primary outcomes; historical versions were not inspected.

## Improved risk markers are a real finding with a defined scope

A later report from **the same experiment** found better LDL cholesterol, blood pressure and insulin-sensitivity measures after two years. These were exploratory risk-factor analyses; fewer cardiovascular events were not demonstrated. Average weight loss was about **7.5 kg**. Adjustment for weight change does not create a separately randomized, weight-matched comparison isolating restriction from weight loss. NIH funds supported the research. [Kraus and colleagues, original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31303390/)

The goal and comparator matter. Choosing weight loss for a defined reason and rebuilding training capacity may require different decisions. A favorable marker alone does not settle that choice.

## A biological-aging estimate is informative without being a lifespan result

In a later analysis of stored blood from **197 participants**, restriction modestly slowed **DunedinPACE**, a DNA-methylation aging-rate estimate. The authors interpreted the change as approximately **2–3%**. PhenoAge and GrimAge were null. Assays were blinded and the analysis used a **P < .005** threshold. No lifespan benefit was measured. Public and charitable funds supported the work; two authors were inventors on DunedinPACE, licensed commercially. A subsequent correction concerned an author affiliation. [Waziry and colleagues, original methods and results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10148951/), [author correction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10275750/)

The practical value depends on whether changing this marker predicts useful long-term outcomes. A commercial biological-age result alone cannot measure my diet's benefit or cost.

## Bone findings belong beside the favorable biomarkers

The bone report found lower bone mineral density at the **lumbar spine, total hip and femoral neck** after restriction; whole-body and wrist results were null. Spine and hip changes were approximately 2%. These were secondary outcomes with repeated-measures and gatekeeping analyses. Both groups received **1,000 mg supplemental calcium and a multivitamin supplying 1,000 IU vitamin D daily**. Supplement provision did not guarantee preservation of these measurements. Increased fractures were not established. Public funds supported the work, with an NIH representative on the steering committee. [Villareal and colleagues, original report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26332798/)

These findings matter when evaluating prolonged weight loss. They do not identify my shoulder pain's cause. The [bone-loading investigation](/nutrition/research/calcium-vitamin-d-and-bone-loading/) develops physical loading and clinical assessment separately.

## Physical function changes depend on which quantity is compared

The fitness report, in participants averaging **38 years**, found **lower absolute oxygen uptake and absolute knee strength**, alongside **higher values relative to body weight**. Treadmill time and workload increased; oxygen uptake relative to lean mass did not change. The intervention contained no structured exercise program. These results come from the same trial, rather than an independent replication in trained lifters. They cannot establish that restriction preserves the absolute force needed for my lifting or improves sprint performance. [Racette and colleagues, original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29045325/)

Absolute and relative outcomes can diverge when weight changes. The [protein and training investigation](/nutrition/research/protein-energy-and-training/) separates lean-mass measurements from function. Name the performance outcome I care about.

## A new anemia analysis adds context to the earlier safety findings

A **2026 analysis of the same trial** reported no group-by-time differences in iron-status, anemia or hepcidin markers. Anemia prevalence remained above 5% in both groups. Everyone received a multivitamin/mineral supplement containing **18 mg iron**, and surveillance could trigger medical assessment or restriction discontinuation. The average biomarker result and individual safety withdrawals can coexist. Full methods were unverified here; the abstract cannot establish safety without that supplementation and surveillance. [Dugan and colleagues, original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41825741/)

The [iron guide](/nutrition/guide/iron/) develops my food estimate. Dietary totals cannot determine whether anemia is present or its cause. Trial supplement provision supplies no personal reason to take that dose.

## What this changes about my current food choices

The research makes deliberate calorie restriction a question about several outcomes: metabolic risk, body weight, bone, function and an unconfirmed lifespan benefit. Multiple papers here analyze **one trial**, so their number should not be mistaken for independent replication.

For the current hotel routine, an enjoyable, repeatable amount of food remains the useful starting point. Record appetite and training context, then add a consistent weight trend if feasible. If intentional weight loss becomes a goal, evaluate it against performance, recovery and the practical ability to eat enough; a biological-aging score alone supplies a weak reason to begin.
