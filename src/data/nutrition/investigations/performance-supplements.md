## Match the supplement to the task

My routine combines short track intervals and strength training. I already have **200 mg caffeine capsules**; actual use is not recorded in the latest food log. Before adding anything, the question should be specific: more repeatable high-intensity work, an extra repetition, a faster sustained effort or better recovery? Those outcomes are not interchangeable, and none of these supplements diagnoses or treats my shoulder.

| Option | Main reason to consider it | Time scale | Practical priority here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Creatine monohydrate | Repeated high-intensity work and training adaptation | Daily use over weeks | A reasonable optional experiment |
| Caffeine | Acute performance or alertness | A particular session | Timing and sleep come first |
| Dietary nitrate | Some endurance/economy protocols | Acute or several days | Optional; not a required food addition |
| Beta-alanine | Fatigue resistance in certain intense tasks | Weeks of loading | Lower priority without a matching test |

This priority order is a practical judgment for my reported routine, not a ranking of universal benefits.

## Creatine: a stronger fit for repeated high-intensity work

Creatine supports the phosphocreatine system that helps rapidly regenerate ATP. **Monohydrate** is the well-studied form; a premium alternative is not needed to reproduce its evidence base. A common sports-nutrition protocol uses **3–5 g daily**; a loading phase speeds muscle-store saturation but is optional. Starting directly with the smaller daily dose takes roughly three to four weeks. It is not an acute stimulant. [ISSN position stand, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5469049/).

In a classic **19-man resistance-training trial**, a week of 25 g/day followed by 5 g/day during 12 weeks of training produced greater strength gains: bench press increased **24% versus 16%**, squat **32% versus 24%**. It also increased muscle-fiber area more than placebo. This supports the possibility of better training adaptation, not a guaranteed personal effect of that size. [Volek et al., 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10449017/).

Lean mass needs careful interpretation. A **2025 randomized study of 63 adults** used 5 g/day and measured participants after a seven-day supplement-only period, then 12 weeks of training. There was an early **0.51 kg** between-group lean-mass difference, but no significant additional training-period difference (**P = .71**). The control received no placebo. This illustrates why an early scale or DXA increase is not automatically new muscle; it does not establish that higher dosing would work better. [Desai et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40292479/).

A small kidney-filtration trial was reassuring within its tested population. The [laboratory-test investigation](/nutrition/research/supplements-and-laboratory-tests/#creatine-presents-a-different-interpretation-problem) retains its methods, attrition and interpretation limits.

## Caffeine: judge the session and the night together

The 2021 ISSN review finds acute benefits in many, though not all, exercise studies. **3–6 mg/kg** is a commonly studied range; some effects appear at lower doses, while very high doses bring more side effects without being necessary. These are research ranges, not a reason to multiply my capsules. [ISSN caffeine position stand](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7777221/).

One small crossover trial in **11 resistance-trained adults** used 5 mg/kg an hour before exercises at 60% of maximum. Average repetitions to failure were **19.6 versus 18.5** with placebo. Perceived exertion and muscle pain were lower too. A reduced sensation of effort or pain is not evidence that an injured tissue can safely tolerate more load. [Duncan et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23834545/).

Sleep is the major tradeoff for late training. In **23 men**, a randomized crossover study tested 100 or 400 mg at four, eight or twelve hours before bedtime. The 100 mg conditions had no significant sleep differences; **400 mg affected sleep even twelve hours before bed**, with greater disruption closer to bedtime. Participants did not always perceive the objectively measured effects. The study did **not test 200 mg**, so neither result gives my capsule a universal safe cutoff. [Gardiner et al., 2024/2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11985402/).

For my evening sessions, preserving sleep may be more valuable than an acute performance boost. Count capsules, coffee, tea and other sources together, and record timing alongside sleep rather than assuming that feeling able to fall asleep settles the question.

## Nitrate: a plausible endurance aid with inconsistent results

Nitrate from beetroot or leafy vegetables can be converted through oral bacteria to nitrite and nitric oxide. In **nine club-level male cyclists**, about **6.2 mmol nitrate** in beetroot juice 2.5 hours before a test improved 4 km and 16.1 km times by **2.8% and 2.7%** versus nitrate-depleted juice. [Lansley et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21471821/).

In **20 well-trained male cyclists**, a higher **8.7 mmol** acute dose increased the blood nitrite marker but did not significantly improve an approximately one-hour time trial. More biomarker response or more nitrate did not ensure a faster performance. Different training status and tests also prevent interpreting this as a simple dose comparison. [Cermak et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22805107/).

Ordinary vegetables remain worthwhile foods without a performance claim. An unassayed beet drink cannot be assigned the trial's nitrate dose from its juice volume alone. Its cost, taste, stomach tolerance and the relevance of a sustained time trial make it a lower-priority experiment than a requirement for my shopping list.

## Beta-alanine: chronic buffering, not immediate tingling-powered performance

Beta-alanine raises muscle carnosine, contributing to intracellular buffering. The ISSN describes **4–6 g/day for at least two to four weeks**, with the clearest relevance to certain intense tasks lasting around one to four minutes. Tingling can occur and can be reduced by smaller divided doses or sustained-release products; it is not evidence the supplement is improving a workout. [ISSN beta-alanine position stand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26175657/).

In a **13-man study**, 6.4 g/day for four weeks improved a sustained knee-extension hold by **13.2%**, without improving maximal voluntary force. In **16 highly trained cyclists**, four weeks did not significantly improve four-minute cycling performance (**P = .25**), though some isokinetic outcomes improved. Capacity in a particular laboratory task is not the same as race performance or maximal strength. [Sale et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3420321/), [Howe et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23630052/).

Ten-second sprints with rests are not equivalent to one continuous effort merely because the session totals a few minutes. Until there is a matching performance question, beta-alanine adds daily complexity without a clear priority.

## Make an experiment interpretable

If I try one option, choose a plain product with a stated dose, change one variable at a time, and record the same training endpoint along with sleep and tolerability. Independent product testing reduces contamination risk but cannot guarantee efficacy or absolute safety. Avoid a blend whose ingredients make both dose and causation unclear. [IOC supplement consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867441/).

Food, appropriate training and sleep remain the foundation. No supplement is recorded as adopted here, and doses above describe established study or consensus protocols rather than a personalized prescription.
