## What would count as better recovery?

Ginger, turmeric and pineapple are interesting foods. The useful question is whether a particular preparation makes a noticeable difference to soreness, restores performance, or improves healing. Those outcomes can disagree. A lower pain rating is a real benefit if it matters to the person experiencing it; it does not by itself establish that a tendon has repaired or that more training is appropriate.

Most exercise trials deliberately produce soreness through unfamiliar, muscle-lengthening exercise. They do not identify the cause of my previously reported, progressively uncomfortable front-of-shoulder pain. The [connective-tissue investigation](/nutrition/research/collagen-broth-and-connective-tissue/) treats that clinical question separately. No ginger, curcumin or bromelain use has been reported in my food log.

## Ginger has a soreness signal, with important limits

Two **2010 randomized, blinded experiments analyzed 34 and 40 volunteers** after **2 g daily of dried ginger**, unheated or heat-treated, for eleven days. Pain 24 hours after unfamiliar elbow-flexor exercise was about **25% and 23% lower**, respectively, than with placebo. These were completer analyses; one placebo participant was excluded after little exercise-induced force loss. Strength, range of motion, swelling and the measured prostaglandin response showed no significant treatment-by-time effects. Separate experiments do not establish a heating advantage. The work was funded by the McCormick Science Institute. This tests specific soreness, not diagnosed injury healing. [Black et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20418184/).

Timing also changes the question. A separate **27-person crossover study** found that a single **2 g ginger dose** did not improve pain, dysfunction or swelling **45 minutes after ingestion**. A favorable next-day finding in a subset did not make it an effective immediate pain reliever. [Black and O’Connor, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21031618/).

A newer **2026 trial analyzed 32 CrossFit practitioners** after nine days of **2 g ginger powder daily**, compared with cornstarch capsules. Ginger improved baseline-adjusted performance in a ten-minute repetition test and reduced perceived effort. One jump-force outcome also favored ginger; other jump measures did not. Many outcomes were tested, without a specified overall correction. Two placebo participants were excluded for nonadherence. There was no delayed-soreness or tissue-healing outcome, and abstract and body give different repetition means, limiting a precise promised benefit. [CrossFit trial, 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13510715/).

The paper calls its registration prospective, but the cited registry describes **acute supplementation during treadmill exercise in a crossover design**, with a different ginger dose and population. It also records first enrollment before registration. That record cannot establish prospective specification of these CrossFit outcomes. [Original ReBEC record](https://ensaiosclinicos.gov.br/rg/RBR-8x3tmqc).

There is contemporary counterevidence in another exercise setting. In **30 trained cyclists**, four weeks of daily shots containing **53 g juiced ginger** did not significantly improve a 75 km time trial or immediately measured soreness. The comparator was ginger ale matched for energy and carbohydrate, containing some ginger extract. The trial was industry funded and not powered to exclude small performance effects. Its soreness measurement was immediately before and after cycling; it does not directly repeat the delayed-soreness experiment above. [Kurtz et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13119850/).

## Curcumin: a positive trial is not a universal result

In **17 men**, a blinded crossover trial using a reported **2.5 g curcumin twice daily** around eccentric leg exercise found less pain during several movements at 24–48 hours, roughly **1–2 points on a ten-point scale**. Some jump comparisons improved, while swelling and pressure tenderness were inconclusive. The authors used magnitude-based inference with **90% confidence limits**, and called for research on translation to sport and clinical populations. This high-dose preparation is not a spoonful of turmeric in dinner. [Nicol et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25795285/).

A **2025 crossover trial in 44 moderately active adults**, with 34 men in its primary analysis, tested **300 mg of a formulated turmeric suspension daily** for five days, supplying at least 90 mg curcuminoids. **The planned soreness outcome was null**, including soreness over 72 hours; some strength/work measures favored turmeric at 24 hours. A favorable percentage-recovery calculation was explicitly **post hoc**. Published analyses also omitted the planned pre-exercise covariate because it was measured after initial dosing. Many outcomes and timepoints were tested, and exercise caused less soreness than expected. The manufacturer funded the study and participated in design and writing. These are preparation-specific exploratory findings, not a successful primary soreness endpoint or evidence about culinary turmeric. [Schönenberger et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12498372/).

The registry was posted before this trial began and lists soreness as primary, with function, power and other measures secondary. Prospective registration is useful, but it does not make a later added outcome confirmatory. [Original registered outcomes](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04946981).

Sport-specific results can also be null: **15 collegiate soccer players** taking **180 mg curcumin daily** around two matched games had no significant improvement in soreness, jump performance or the measured damage/inflammation markers versus placebo. [Tanabe et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38436665/).

Nor should an “anti-inflammatory” mechanism be assumed from the ingredient name. A **2026 analysis of 26 young adults** given **1,000 mg daily of a formulation containing about 200 mg curcumin** found higher circulating metabolites, but no consistent anti-inflammatory or antioxidant pattern after jumping exercise. Some markers were lower, others higher or unchanged. This was a further analysis of an earlier trial cohort, not another independent replication, and it did not measure injury healing. [Alhebshi et al., 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s44410-026-00026-w).

## Pineapple and bromelain are different exposures

In a blinded trial, **39 participants completed** an elbow-exercise experiment comparing bromelain, ibuprofen, placebo and no treatment. **Bromelain 300 mg three times daily**, started after exercise, did not improve soreness, lost range of motion or lost peak torque. [Stone et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12466693/).

A small stage-race trial in **15 trained cyclists** used **1,000 mg bromelain daily**. Fatigue was lower on day four, but this selected perceptual finding did not establish improved muscle-damage markers or injury healing. [Shing et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25604346/).

A wisdom-tooth surgery trial analyzed **42 patients**, after seven exclusions for missed follow-ups or nonstudy drugs. Standardized freeze-dried pineapple extract and purified bromelain both reduced rescue ibuprofen use and reported pain versus placebo; restricted mouth opening did not significantly improve. Both active preparations supplied measured bromelain, initially **800 mg daily**, then 400 mg; enzymatic activity was specified for the purified product. This tests postoperative symptoms, not ordinary pineapple or training-injury repair. [Colletti et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10974198/).

The [existing shoulder-surgery discussion](/nutrition/research/collagen-broth-and-connective-tissue/) concerns a multi-ingredient product containing bromelain, collagen and other ingredients. It cannot isolate bromelain's contribution. “Contains the same molecule” is insufficient to equate a fruit, a quantified extract and a combination product.

## What this changes in daily shopping

These trials do not create a requirement to buy an extra food or extract. Ginger can be a flavor option; pineapple can join the [fruit rotation](/nutrition/research/fruit-substitutions/) if I enjoy it and can finish the amount purchased. Neither needs to displace fruit I already like or add another difficult-to-store package solely for recovery.

Concentrated preparations deserve their own assessment. The US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network described **ten high-confidence turmeric-associated cases**, including five hospitalizations and one death. Some products contained piperine. This referral case series cannot calculate the risk per user, establish that piperine caused the injuries, or assign the same risk to culinary turmeric. It does establish that a supplement's plant origin does not guarantee harmlessness. [Original DILIN report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9892270/).

Drug interactions also need actual evidence and the actual product. A **12-man randomized crossover experiment** found no significant effect of its ginger preparation on warfarin exposure, INR or platelet aggregation. A short study in healthy volunteers receiving a single warfarin dose cannot establish safety for every product or someone receiving long-term anticoagulation. Medication use has not been reported here; an extract decision would require that context. [Jiang et al., 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1884814/).

The doses above describe research protocols, not my regimen. The evidence supports a possible, preparation-specific soreness benefit worth studying, alongside real null results. It does not establish faster healing of my shoulder, prevention of injury, or better long-term training adaptation. The [antioxidant investigation](/nutrition/research/antioxidants-and-training/) explains why its high-dose vitamin findings should not be automatically transferred to these foods or extracts.
