## The nutrient table is one view of the meal

I want meals I enjoy, can finish and can buy with the storage available to me. The earlier rice, chicken, beans, olives and fish combination failed that test: I disliked parts of it, became too full and discarded food. A later day was more enjoyable. Those observations matter even before asking whether a table reaches a particular nutrient reference.

The food table answers questions about estimated amounts. A dietary pattern asks what foods replace other foods, how a meal is prepared, how often I eat it and whether I can sustain it. A food's physical structure also affects chewing and digestion. Neither view replaces the other. Reaching a vitamin reference does not prove that a diet improves training or long-term health; a named diet does not tell me whether today's protein or energy intake fits my needs.

## What the corrected PREDIMED trial supports

The **2018 corrected PREDIMED report** studied **7,447 adults in Spain** at high cardiovascular risk: men aged 55–80 and women 60–80. Median follow-up was **4.8 years**. Major cardiovascular events occurred in **3.8%** with Mediterranean-diet/olive-oil advice, **3.4%** with Mediterranean-diet/nuts advice and **4.4%** with reduced-fat advice; adjusted hazard ratios were **0.69** and **0.72**. [Estruch et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389).

The original report was withdrawn after randomization departures, including household and clinic assignments. Corrected analyses adjusted for these problems; results were similar after excluding 1,588 participants with known or suspected departures.

Participants received advice and supplied foods; control counseling was initially less intensive. Public funding and food donations were disclosed; sponsors reported no role in design, analysis or reporting. This supports a dietary strategy in that population without isolating a nut or herb, predicting my training outcomes or prescribing the trial’s oil quantities.

## Processing can affect intake beyond the offered nutrient totals

Hall's inpatient crossover trial gave **20 adults** an ultra-processed diet and an unprocessed diet for **two weeks each**, in random order. Participants could eat freely. The offered diets were designed to match several nutrient totals, but actual intake was about **508 kcal/day higher** on the ultra-processed diet. Participants gained about **0.9 kg** during that phase and lost about **0.9 kg** during the other. [Hall et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7946062/).

This is experimental evidence that those two menus changed eating behavior. It does not identify a universal toxic ingredient or prove that equal calories consumed would have different effects on weight. The diets also differed in eating rate and the energy density of their non-beverage foods. Matching a menu's offered fiber and macronutrients did not match all its physical properties or the amounts ultimately eaten.

For my current goal, the implication is broader than avoiding excess intake. A very bulky menu can also make it difficult to eat enough comfortably. Enjoyment, portion size, texture and meal timing belong in the log alongside calories.

## A healthier ultra-processed menu is a useful counterexample

A **2025 community crossover trial** provided two eight-week diets that both followed the UK Eatwell Guide. It randomized **55 adults with overweight or obesity**; 50 contributed primary-outcome data for at least one diet. Average weight changed by **−2.06%** on the minimally processed diet and **−1.05%** on the ultra-processed diet, a **1.01 percentage-point** difference. Thus both menus produced weight loss, with more on the minimally processed one. [Dicken et al., 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03842-0).

The result concerns supplied menus in a predominantly female, higher-BMI population. It does not establish weight loss as my goal or show that every ultra-processed food is equivalent. There was attrition, and the diets differed in more than a category label. Published 2026 correspondence challenges how confidently the weight difference can be attributed to processing itself; the authors published a reply. [Robinson and Forde, 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-04085-9), [Dicken et al., reply](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-04089-5).

## Texture can change intake within the same category

In another crossover experiment, **41 adults** ate two diets supplying roughly **95% of energy from ultra-processed foods** for two weeks each. The menus were matched for several properties, including non-beverage energy density, while their textures encouraged slower or faster eating. Intake was **369 kcal/day lower** on the slower-eating menu. Body weight did not significantly differ between diets. [Forde et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41314613/).

That is evidence for a specific mechanism, not a finding that processing never matters. It shows why the category alone is incomplete: foods can vary in texture, energy density, nutrient content and convenience. The study excluded people doing more than four hours of moderate-to-vigorous activity weekly, so its precise intake difference is not an athlete prediction.

## Structure also changes how a nutrient total is used

An **18-person crossover study** measured **4.42 kcal/g** for whole natural almonds and **6.53 kcal/g** for pure almond butter. These are experimental diet averages, not personal calorie corrections. [Gebauer et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27713968/). The [nut-energy companion](/nutrition/research/whole-nuts-butter-and-measured-energy/) examines processing comparisons and balance measurements.

## What changes in my shopping routine

The useful default is a repeatable set of foods I enjoy, with enough variety to cover ordinary nutrient needs and enough flexibility to adjust energy and meal size. Packaging, freezing, canning or cooking can make that feasible; the presence of a package is not itself a reason to reject rice, yogurt or fish.

I would keep the daily record focused on **what I actually ate, the amounts I can verify, enjoyment, appetite, digestion and discarded food**. A proposed change should specify its purpose: a more enjoyable meal, easier preparation, a nutrient source or a training-related outcome. That is more informative than maximizing a processing score or collecting foods because each has one promising paper.
