My [vitamin A guide](/nutrition/guide/vitamin-a/) separates the dated intake estimate from unassessed body status.

## What avocado changed

In a randomized crossover, 12 healthy adults ate **300 g raw carrots**, with or without **150 g avocado**. The avocado meal added 23 g fat and 275 kcal to a breakfast initially containing 2 g fat. Over 12 hours, the blood transport fraction showed 6.6 times greater β-carotene exposure and 12.6 times greater retinyl-ester exposure, a vitamin A product. These are exposure ratios, **not a twelvefold improvement in the fraction converted**: estimated mean conversion was 27% versus 34%, with considerable variation. A separate tomato experiment used a specially bred high-carotene sauce, not my ordinary tomato. Hass Avocado Board supported this unequal-calorie comparison. It does not establish an avocado-specific advantage over other fats or improved long-term vitamin A status. [Kopec and colleagues, 2014](https://doi.org/10.3945/jn.113.187674)

## Processing can affect uptake and conversion differently

Nine adults received commercial carrot purée and boiled-mashed carrots containing the same β-carotene dose, with an isotope reference in oil; six also received a raw preparation. Purée produced roughly twice the carotene uptake of boiled-mashed carrots, but the retinol-yield comparison was inconclusive (**P = 0.11**). The raw preparation was intermediate and not clearly different. This Gerber-supported study gives a useful counterexample to equating better carotene uptake with proportionately more vitamin A. Its abstract and indexed original methods were accessible; the full publisher text could not be directly retrieved. [Edwards and colleagues, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/132.2.159)

A randomized crossover recruited five people and analyzed four, comparing **100 g labelled carrots** with or without stir-frying in 10.5 mL oil. Both meals also contained oil-based isotope references. The preparation called “raw” had already been blanched, homogenized and frozen. Isotope modelling estimated higher carotene bioavailability after stir-frying, but cooking and added oil changed together. The model’s retinol equivalents also differed; altered reference-carotene absorption could explain this. This Medical Research Council-funded proof-of-method study cannot isolate heat from fat, establish a universal conversion factor, or predict my response to a fresh carrot or a microwaved vegetable. [Ghavami and colleagues, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1017/S000711451100451X)

## The recent juice result has an unusual meal

A 2025 paper describes a crossover in 16 adults aged 20–30. It compared **320 g carrots with 530 g juice**, each supplying 25 mg β-carotene and accompanied by **50 mL canola oil**. Juice produced about twice the plasma β-carotene exposure; retinol and the antioxidant assays did not show a corresponding benefit. Period timing, participant accounting and crossover analysis are insufficiently described, and several outcomes and time points were tested. The authors report no conflicts; funding and a trial registration are not identified in the accessible paper. This supports a preparation effect under those conditions, not a health advantage from buying juice or adding that much oil. [Choi and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.4162/nrp.2025.19.2.215)

## A small shopping experiment

My existing food already includes avocado, nuts and chimichurri. I can try the [guide’s carrot option](/nutrition/guide/vitamin-a/) with my existing meal as a manageable produce swap, choosing an amount I can finish and recording its edible portion and preparation. I can also revisit the [fruit choices](/nutrition/research/fruit-substitutions/) if I prefer mango or orange-fleshed cantaloupe.

I have not reported adopting this swap. I would keep taste, appetite and the storage situation in the decision, and avoid adding another large food pile.

These carrot experiments do not measure mango or cantaloupe conversion. They justify neither extra oil to chase blood responses nor multiplying my logged vitamin A by their uptake ratios.
