## The practical question

The basmati pouch makes a simple chicken meal possible with a microwave. That convenience matters in the current hotel routine. A useful quality question is whether repeatedly relying on one grain changes contaminant exposure, and whether a realistic substitution or preparation would improve it. No arsenic measurement is available for my actual pouch, and none is added to the [food record](/nutrition/record/) as a guessed value.

Rice can accumulate arsenic from its growing environment. FDA advises a varied diet and says rice can remain part of a balanced eating pattern. That supports considering a grain rotation; it does not require discarding a food that makes the routine work. [FDA practical guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/environmental-contaminants-food/what-you-can-do-limit-exposure-arsenic).

## A product name is not a matching test

A **2025 HBBF report** commissioned laboratory tests of **145 rice samples and 66 other grain samples**. Its appendix lists two **dry Trader Joe's Organic Basmati Rice from India** samples with **29.6 and 35.1 µg/kg inorganic arsenic**. The adjacent **52.7 and 44.5 µg/kg** figures are *total* arsenic, a different measure. These are not identified as my frozen microwave product. The report gives dry-grain concentrations for these samples and wet-weight concentrations for ready-to-heat products. Comparing those columns directly would confuse concentration with the water added during preparation. This commissioned survey is original measurement evidence, rather than a clinical trial or a guarantee for later batches. Its methods describe laboratory quality controls; the individual laboratory reports and samples have not been independently reanalyzed here. [Original report, Appendix A pp.29–30 and Appendix B pp.38–39](https://hbbf.org/sites/default/files/2025-05/Arsenic-in-Rice-Report_May2025_R6_SECURED.pdf).

For a future product comparison, identify the exact product, batch if available, arsenic species, preparation state and measurement units. Then consider the amount eaten and how often. A shared brand name cannot fill the missing measurement. The same distinction matters in the [cocoa](/nutrition/research/cocoa-contaminants-and-serving-size/) and [seafood](/nutrition/research/seafood-contaminants-and-species/) investigations.

## Cooking studies describe a different starting food

FDA researchers tested rinsing and varying cooking-water amounts. Their original report found average inorganic-arsenic reductions of **40% in polished long-grain rice, 60% in parboiled rice and 50% in brown rice** when cooking with excess water. But **50–70% of enriched iron, folate, niacin and thiamine** was also lost from enriched polished and parboiled rice. Rinsing had little effect on arsenic in this experiment and removed added nutrients. Only the original abstract was accessible here, so sample selection and the full statistical analysis have not been independently inspected. These results concern rice being cooked; they do not demonstrate that reheating a precooked pouch removes arsenic or that my rice was enriched. [Gray et al., original study, published online 2015 and in the 2016 issue](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26515534/).

A later laboratory study selected **six UK rice products**, three white and three brown, with relatively high initial inorganic-arsenic concentrations. Its two-stage method briefly boiled rice, discarded that water and finished cooking in fresh water. Average inorganic-arsenic removal was **73% for white rice and 54% for brown rice**. The investigators measured selected mineral elements, not every nutrient; zinc did not show a significant loss, while other retention results varied. Cooking replicates are repeated preparations of these products, rather than a representative survey of all rice. The paper's exposure calculation used older reference assumptions and is not adopted as a current personal risk estimate. [Menon et al., 2021, original methods and results](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/167758/1/1-s2.0-S0048969720368728-main.pdf).

The **2024 follow-up** compared excess-water cooking with that two-stage method in white, brown and parboiled basmati. With low-arsenic tap water, both removed about **54–58%** of inorganic arsenic from white and brown rice; parboiled rice differed between methods. Some minerals were lost. When cooking water was deliberately spiked with arsenic, rice retained more arsenic, especially with the two-stage method. This tests how rice and water interact under specified laboratory conditions. It does not establish a universal removal percentage, a clinical benefit or a treatment for a factory-cooked pouch. [Menon et al., 2024, original study](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/232354/1/1-s2.0-S0048969723082839-main.pdf).

## Exposure references answer different questions

The same-looking number can have a different purpose:

| Assessment | Reference | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EFSA, 2024 | **0.06 µg inorganic arsenic/kg body weight/day** | A reference point from the lower confidence bound of a modeled dose associated with a 5% *relative* increase over background skin-cancer incidence. |
| US EPA IRIS, January 2025 | **0.06 µg/kg body weight/day** | A chronic oral reference dose based on noncancer outcomes, particularly ischemic heart disease and type 2 diabetes. |

EFSA used a margin-of-exposure approach for this genotoxic carcinogen and did **not** establish a health-based guidance value or a margin defined as low concern. Its reference point is not a nutrient UL or a safe rice allowance. [Original EFSA assessment, summary and conclusions](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/351262079/EFSA_Journal_-_2024_-_-_Update_of_the_risk_assessment_of_inorganic_arsenic_in_food.pdf).

EPA's reference dose includes uncertainty and concerns repeated lifetime exposure; cancer assessment is separate. It is not a product concentration limit, and an amount below it does not establish zero cancer risk. The two references' equal rounded values do not make their endpoints or derivations interchangeable. [Original EPA executive summary, pp.2–4](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0278_summary.pdf).

FDA's **100 µg/kg action level** applies to **infant rice cereal**, including organic and conventional products. It should not be turned into an adult daily intake limit or a pass/fail threshold for this microwave pouch. [FDA action-level scope](https://www.fda.gov/food/chemical-metals-natural-toxins-pesticides-guidance-documents-regulations/supporting-document-action-level-inorganic-arsenic-rice-cereals-infants).

## What I would do with this information

Keep the convenient pouch as an available meal component. Try a different starch occasionally if a palatable, same-day option is easy to obtain and prepare. The next choice should still solve appetite and storage rather than add another unfinished package. I would not switch automatically to brown rice solely because its nutrient table looks richer, or assign an exposure number from a different product's test.

When a kitchen becomes available, preparation studies can inform a specific comparison. For now, there is no practical reason to reconstruct laboratory cooking around a product that is already cooked. Better product-specific monitoring would change the assessment; the missing measurement remains a limit on what can be concluded.
