## The useful question is what changes in a particular diet

I enjoy walnuts and almonds. It would take meaningful human evidence to conclude that removing them improves my diet simply because they contain omega-6. “Seed oil” also groups oils with very different fatty-acid compositions. One recent trial compared two varieties of safflower oil: both came from seeds, but one contained far more linoleic acid than the other. Food category, fatty-acid dose and replacement food need separate attention. [Sergeant and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18111814)

Linoleic acid, **LA**, is an essential omega-6 fatty acid. The US reference for men aged 19–30 is an **AI of 17 g/day**; a separate **AMDR of 5–10% of energy** concerns n-6 PUFA, chiefly LA. These were set under uncertainty, and neither is a clinical deficiency cutoff or a toxicity threshold. [Original essential-fat reference, printed p.464](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10490/chapter/10), [Original distribution-range chapter, printed pp.825–826](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10490/chapter/13)

## Why my food record cannot yet give a complete LA total

USDA separates specific **18:2 n-6 cis,cis** LA from broader **18:2 undifferentiated**, which can combine positional or geometric isomers. In the original SR28 records matched to my 19-food catalog, only almonds have the specific LA measurement. Several foods have substantial broader 18:2 values, but replacing one identity with another would conceal the missing measurements. These are generic food comparators, not analyses of my purchases. [USDA source definitions, printed pages 23–24](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400535/Data/SR/sr28/sr28_doc.pdf), [Original USDA archive](https://www.ars.usda.gov/northeast-area/beltsville-md-bhnrc/beltsville-human-nutrition-research-center/methods-and-application-of-food-composition-laboratory/mafcl-site-pages/sr11-sr28/)

My walnuts, chicken and sauce cannot become zero simply because specific values are missing. The restaurant sauce's oil type and quantity are also unverified, and generic oil-packed fish may use a different packing oil from my can. Adding the few specific values would therefore give an **incomplete available subtotal**, not my daily intake. This source gap does not establish that I need more fat or have an essential-fat deficiency. LA is included within total fat and calories; it would never be added to them a second time. ## Actual human trials do not support a simple inflammatory rule

In **DIVAS**, 195 adults aged 21–60 with moderate cardiovascular risk were assigned to three 16-week diets. Saturated fat was replaced with predominantly monounsaturated fat or n-6 PUFA, while intended energy intake stayed the same. The n-6 substitution lowered LDL cholesterol by about **13.6%**, but did **not** improve the primary endpoint, flow-mediated dilation. The reported E-selectin benefit belonged to the monounsaturated arm. LDL changes were measured; projected mortality reductions were calculations, not observed deaths prevented. [Vafeiadou and colleagues, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26016869/)

A **2026 pilot** compared foods delivering soybean oil with foods delivering palm oil in a randomized crossover. It enrolled only **10 adults with overweight or obesity**; each period lasted four weeks. There were no significant between-treatment changes in the reported systemic inflammatory markers or oxidized LDL. Some assays had only six to ten available samples. A small, short null result cannot establish equivalence or universal safety. Soy funding and donated oil are disclosed. [Yang and colleagues, 2026, including the original tables](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12906016/)

That pilot's other fatty-acid findings are also relevant: red-cell DHA fell during the soybean-oil period, with a between-treatment difference. This does not establish worse brain health or recovery; it is a composition change. The paper reuses the cohort from an earlier feasibility report, so it is **one small experiment**, not two independent replications. [Same trial's primary feasibility report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41461275/)

## A new trial strengthens a narrower metabolic concern

The June 2026 safflower-oil study randomized **80 adults** and retained **52 through 12 weeks**. Achieved LA intake was approximately **2.65% versus 10.21% of energy**. The lower-LA diet replaced much of the LA with oleic acid; ALA exposure was similar between arms. Higher LA lowered plasma EPA and increased several arachidonic-acid-to-EPA and stimulated-blood lipid-mediator ratios. The paper adjusted primary and secondary comparisons for multiplicity. Arachidonic acid itself did not rise with the higher intake. [Sergeant and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18111814)

The inflammatory challenge occurred **in a blood sample outside the body**. Changes in these responses establish altered biochemistry, without establishing more cardiovascular events, worse recovery or inflammatory disease. They warrant further trials rather than a personalized elimination target. [Full original study](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC13259401), [Registered biochemical outcomes](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02962128)

Plasma EPA, stimulated cell responses, circulating CRP and disease outcomes are different measurements. A universal “inflammatory” label hides that distinction.

## People may respond differently, but the evidence is not a diet prescription

**FADSDIET2** randomized 130 Finnish men, mostly in their mid-60s, to eight-week sunflower or camelina diets; 118 completed. Both diets increased fat intake. Among 37 sunflower participants retained after medicine/high-CRP exclusions, the registered secondary CRP marker showed a genotype-by-time response difference. Neither genotype's separate within-group change was significant. These are distinct statistical comparisons, and multiple outcomes were examined. This is a reason to study differences in fatty-acid metabolism, not an established case for genetic testing or for predicting my response at 19–30. Later papers using the same participants are follow-up analyses, not fresh trials. [Lankinen and colleagues, 2021](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mnfr.202001004)

## Current policy and practical choices

The 2025–2030 US Scientific Foundation calls for more research separating whole-food nuts and seeds from refined and thermally stressed oils. That policy discussion does not establish that my walnut portions cause inflammation. [Scientific Foundation, printed page 35](https://cdn.realfood.gov/Scientific%20Report_508.pdf)

The [nutrient guide](/nutrition/guide/) places current food-policy references beside the distinct nutrient requirements.

For my present daily-shopping pattern, **keep enjoyable nut portions and palatable oily fish**, and clarify the restaurant sauce if changing energy or fat is useful. An unknown oil recipe is a measurement problem. I do not need an additional bottle of oil or an elaborate avoidance routine to act on these studies. Research on isolated oils does not measure the full effect of walnuts, and trials of ordinary dietary substitutions do not settle every question about repeatedly heated frying oils. Improving the diet still means comparing a concrete replacement, appetite, total portions and sustained outcomes—not assigning every omega-6-containing food one health label.
