My current record includes ownership of 200 mg caffeine capsules and a plan to buy vitamin D. It does not establish a caffeine dose actually taken, and vitamin D was explicitly missed on the latest reported day. Before adding supplements to the nutrition totals, the useful information is the actual bottle, its formulation, the amount per serving, and how much I took.

Product quality answers whether the intended material is present. Human research answers what that material did in a studied population. Both are useful; neither substitutes for the other.

## Start with the amount per actual unit

The **Supplement Facts** serving can contain more than one capsule. A label saying 200 mg per two-capsule serving is different from 200 mg per capsule. Count the units actually taken before entering the dose. Proprietary blends can list a combined weight without the amounts of their individual non-DV ingredients; the total blend weight cannot be assigned to caffeine, an amino acid, or an extract within it. [FDA supplement-labeling guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/dietary-supplement-labeling-guide-chapter-iv-nutrition-labeling).

Several label distinctions matter for this project:

| Label detail | What belongs in the record |
| --- | --- |
| Vitamin D | Amount in µg and/or IU, D2 or D3, serving size, and units taken |
| Magnesium | Elemental magnesium amount and its stated source form |
| Fish oil | Oil amount and separately declared EPA, DHA or omega-3 total |
| Caffeine | Caffeine amount per serving, serving units and other caffeine sources |
| Botanical extract | Plant and part, extract identity and any declared standardized constituent |

The magnesium amount in Supplement Facts refers to **elemental magnesium**, not the entire salt's weight. A front-label number for a magnesium complex cannot simply replace that amount. [NIH magnesium label explanation](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/).

Likewise, **1,000 mg fish oil is not necessarily 1,000 mg EPA plus DHA**. NIH describes a common formulation with 180 mg EPA and 120 mg DHA within 1,000 mg oil, while emphasizing substantial product variation. The actual label is needed. An unspecified omega-3 total should remain separate from a verified EPA/DHA split. [NIH omega-3 formulations](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/).

An extract amount can also differ from the amount of a named constituent. For example, an extract standardized to a stated percentage has a different identity from a food powder of the same weight. That distinction is needed before comparing either with a trial formulation; it is not evidence that a concentrated extract works better.

## FDA regulation does not mean every bottle was pretested

US dietary supplements are regulated, and manufacturers have obligations concerning manufacturing quality, safety and truthful labeling. FDA generally does **not approve supplements or their labels before marketing**, and does not routinely test every product before sale. It can inspect firms, test selected products, review notifications and take enforcement action. “FDA registered facility” therefore does not mean that FDA approved a particular bottle's contents or clinical benefit. [FDA explanation of manufacturer and regulator responsibilities](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements).

A label database serves another purpose. NIH's Dietary Supplement Label Database preserves current and historical label information, but inclusion is not an endorsement or an independent guarantee that the measured contents match. It is helpful for recovering a formulation; it is not an assay of the bottle I own. [NIH label-database disclaimer](https://dsld.od.nih.gov/).

## Original measurements show why this distinction matters

A 2013 study sampled vitamin D3 pills from 15 sealed retail bottles bought at five stores, then tested additional same-lot and different-lot bottles and compounded pills. In the initial retail-bottle analysis, individual pills contained **52%–135%** of the expected dose. Across the additional different-lot retail testing, the range was **9%–140%**. One USP-verified manufacturer's 5,000 IU formulation was comparatively consistent, while its 1,000 IU formulation was more variable in some comparisons. [Original vitamin D potency study](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1570096).

This was a small historical sample, not a 2026 market survey or a finding about my unreported vitamin D brand. It demonstrates that amount declared and amount measured can differ. It also cautions against promising that a quality mark makes every individual pill exact. The research's high-dose products were analytical samples, not doses proposed for me.

## Certification programs have different scopes

**USP Verified** includes product testing, manufacturing and documentation review, contaminant specifications, and performance testing such as proper dissolution or disintegration. It also uses ongoing audits and off-the-shelf testing. These tests support quality and release of the ingredient; they do not establish a clinical benefit or comprehensive freedom from side effects and interactions. [USP verification explanation](https://test.usp.org/frequently-asked-questions/usp-verification-services), [USP mark summary](https://www.usp.org/sites/default/files/usp/document/products-services/dietary-supplement-verification-summary-final.pdf).

**NSF/ANSI 173 supplement certification** includes label-claim, toxicology and contaminant review, with ongoing facility audits and periodic product retesting. **NSF Certified for Sport** adds screening for substances prohibited by sporting organizations. These are different claims from simply having a manufacturing facility audited. [NSF program scope](https://www.nsf.org/consumer-resources/articles/supplement-vitamin-certification).

**Informed Sport** emphasizes banned-substance testing of each certified product batch before release and provides batch verification. Its scope should not be assumed to prove every nutrient's dose, every possible contaminant, or clinical efficacy merely because the sport mark is present. [Informed Sport testing and batch verification](https://sport.wetestyoutrust.com/about/frequently-asked-questions).

The practical check is the **exact product**, including formulation, strength, flavor or regional variant where applicable, rather than the brand name alone. Use the certification program's own listing, and check the lot when the program supplies lot records. The [NSF Certified for Sport directory](https://www.nsfsport.com/certified-products/) and [USP verified-product resource](https://www.quality-supplements.org/usp_verified_products) provide product searches. This review did not verify a specific bottle I own.

## A certificate of analysis needs a scope

A useful certificate identifies the sample or lot, laboratory, methods, numerical results, test limits and date. FDA's manufacturing guidance specifically requires methods, limits and actual results when a supplier certificate is used under the relevant component-testing provisions. A certificate for a raw ingredient is different from an assay of the finished capsules. [FDA manufacturing and certificate requirements](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/small-entity-compliance-guide-current-good-manufacturing-practice-manufacturing-packaging-labeling).

“99% purity” alone does not establish the amount in each unit, the identity of the remaining material, or results for metals and microbial contamination. A test that did not include an analyte cannot clear that analyte. This is an interpretation of the test's scope, rather than a claim that an untested hazard is present.

The same principle matters even more when reading experimental-compound claims: an ingredient-quality certificate does not establish efficacy, approval for a claimed use, or suitability for another route of administration. The [peptide precision investigation](/nutrition/research/peptide-precision/) keeps those questions separate.

## What changes in my routine

For a simple supplement, the first preference is a clear single-ingredient dose and a checkable quality record, followed by the evidence and reference limits relevant to that ingredient. There is no need to assemble a large supplement stack to make the food log complete.

My caffeine capsules should stay in the “owned; use unreported” category until a dose is reported. Premeasured capsules are a different situation from measuring bulk concentrated powder or liquid: the FDA specifically advises avoiding those bulk products because small measurement errors can be dangerous. A capsule still requires its dose and the day's other caffeine sources to be counted. [FDA concentrated-caffeine advice](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-select-dietary-supplement-ingredients-and-other-substances/fda-warns-consumers-about-pure-and-highly-concentrated-caffeine).

For vitamin D, the [nutrient guide](/nutrition/guide/vitamin-d/) supplies intake and upper-reference context. Product identity and an actual dose taken then belong alongside that context. If laboratory testing is later relevant, the product and timing also help interpret possible interactions described in [supplements and laboratory tests](/nutrition/research/supplements-and-laboratory-tests/).
