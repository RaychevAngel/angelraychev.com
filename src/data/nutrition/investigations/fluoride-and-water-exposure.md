## An omitted exposure needs the right question

My food log does not currently establish fluoride intake. Drinking-water source and quantity, tea consumption, and relevant dental products have not been reported. A blank food-table cell should remain unknown; it is not evidence that I need a fluoride supplement.

Fluoride is useful for preventing tooth decay, but its intake references have a different rationale from a vitamin required for an ordinary biochemical function. The adult male **Adequate Intake is 4 mg/day**, established around caries prevention because evidence was insufficient for an Estimated Average Requirement. The adult **upper intake level is 10 mg/day from all sources**, based on dental and skeletal fluorosis. These references do not create a compulsory daily amount to swallow, nor do they settle every newer question about developmental exposure. [NIH fluoride reference](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Fluoride-HealthProfessional/).

For this notebook, the useful separation is **dental benefit, actual exposure and possible harm at a particular exposure and life stage**. A simple green/red percentage cannot answer all three.

## Daily amount and water concentration are different units

A water measurement in **mg/L** describes concentration. An intake in **mg/day** describes an amount consumed over time. If water contained 0.7 mg/L and a person drank one liter, that water would contribute 0.7 mg; two liters would contribute 1.4 mg. This is arithmetic illustrating the units, not a recommendation for my water intake or a claim about what I drank.

In the San Francisco system's **2025 report, published in 2026**, the treated-water fluoride range was **0.5–0.8 mg/L, averaging 0.7 mg/L**. The main table also has a source-water fluoride row with a different range; that row should not be mistaken for the treated water delivered to customers. The reported California maximum contaminant level is **2.0 mg/L**. These are system measurements and standards, not a personal blood test or a measurement of my hotel tap. [SFPUC 2025 annual report, water data and footnote 4](https://www.sfpuc.gov/sites/default/files/accounts-and-services/water-quality/water-quality-en-SF-2025.pdf).

The current federal maximum contaminant level is **4.0 mg/L**. A legal maximum, a fluoridation recommendation and a daily intake reference serve different purposes; none should be relabeled as the others. As of this review, EPA's current page describes a new toxicity-assessment protocol released **August 3, 2026**, rather than a completed replacement regulation. [EPA's current fluoride assessment page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/fluoride-drinking-water).

## What the developmental evidence says—and leaves unresolved

The National Toxicology Program's 2024 review concluded with moderate confidence that **higher exposure**, such as drinking-water concentrations above 1.5 mg/L, is associated with lower IQ in children. Its current explanation says evidence is insufficient to determine whether water at **0.7 mg/L** affects children's IQ. This is an unresolved lower-exposure question, not proof either of a measurable effect at that concentration or of zero risk for every child. The review concerns neurodevelopment; it does not establish that a young adult has cognitive injury from local tap water. [NTP review and updated explanation](https://ntp.niehs.nih.gov/research/assessments/noncancer/completed/fluoride).

The related 2025 meta-analysis included **74 observational studies**, of which 52 were judged at high risk of bias and 22 at low risk. Water and urinary exposure measures were associated with IQ across the broader literature. The lower-water-exposure analyses had few studies and considerable uncertainty: a negative direction of association does not always mean a statistically clear result. Urinary concentration also reflects exposure differently from water concentration, so a urine-based coefficient cannot be pasted onto a tap-water value. The authors did not claim to resolve the dose–response relation below 1.5 mg/L using drinking-water exposure alone. [Taylor et al., 2025](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2828425).

These studies deserve attention without turning an association into an individual causal diagnosis. They also do not, by themselves, quantify the combined dental benefits and developmental risks of a specific local policy. That broader assessment is precisely where current review work matters.

## What would make the personal record more useful

I can first record what water I actually drink: municipal tap, a named bottled product or another source, with an approximate amount. If I begin drinking substantial tea, its type and quantity also matter; tea leaves can contribute fluoride beyond the brewing water, with considerable variation. A generic food value would give false confidence for an unidentified tea. [NIH sources of fluoride](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Fluoride-HealthProfessional/).

Dental products should be recorded separately from foods. Applying toothpaste is not equivalent to swallowing its entire fluoride content. Oral-care choices and any prescription fluoride should be discussed in terms of dental risk and the product's instructions; the dietary AI does not justify adding a supplement just to fill a table. The local water provider is the appropriate source for water-system measurements. [CDC water-fluoridation questions](https://www.cdc.gov/fluoridation/faq/).

The proposed tracking status is **clarify exposure**, with no clinical deficiency label. The priority is understanding the water and dental context, not adding another item to an already complicated shopping list.
