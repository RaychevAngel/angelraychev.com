## The decision for this routine

I confirmed eating the whole 32 oz yogurt tub, and I have described turning yogurt into ayran to drink throughout the day. The latest food model estimates about 182 g protein, including about 53 g from that yogurt. The amount eaten is confirmed; its composition still relies on a provisional label. Restaurant chicken remains an estimate. Exact eating times are unrecorded, so the described routine does not establish how protein was distributed on that particular day. [Food log](/nutrition/record/)

The useful question is whether changing the arrangement of these foods would improve training enough to justify extra effort. The present evidence gives no compelling reason to add a protein purchase, replace enjoyable dairy with a fast-digesting powder, or create five compulsory protein occasions. Moving some existing food earlier can still make a large evening meal more comfortable. That is a practical option, rather than an established muscle-building requirement. The broader [protein and energy investigation](/nutrition/research/protein-energy-and-training/) covers total intake and the evidence behind proposed per-meal ceilings.

## Does a more even schedule improve actual training results?

A controlled 16-week study prescribed the same 90 g daily protein either as 30/30/30 g across three meals or 10/20/60 g. Participants also followed an energy deficit and resistance training. Among 41 completers from 54 who started, distribution did not significantly change lean-mass, muscle-area or strength responses. This is longer-term evidence using ordinary meals, but it concerns adults with overweight losing weight at much less protein than this food estimate. It does not prove every schedule equivalent. Food-industry boards helped fund the trial. [Hudson trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5657287/)

A 2025 study is closer in protein amount: trained young men consumed three or five substantial protein meals during eight weeks of lifting. The original abstract reports no between-group advantage for muscle size, lean mass or strength. However, only 18 of 32 allocated participants completed the study. The registry describes dietary prescriptions and reported intake, rather than identical provided diets, and was registered after enrollment began. Full published methods were inaccessible in this review. This is a small null result with substantial attrition, rather than a demonstration that three and five meals have identical effects. [Tavares trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40673785/), [original registry](https://ensaiosclinicos.gov.br/rg/RBR-4bnbn3)

The positive-sounding breakfast literature also needs care. In a 12-week study, a supplied 15 g whey serving was placed at breakfast or dinner. The primary total lean-mass comparison missed the study's significance threshold, with P = 0.056; limb lean mass and strength did not show a benefit. Only 26 of 33 assigned men were analyzed, other food was self-reported, and actual daily protein means differed. Despite the paper describing prospective registration, the registry shows registration after the intervention. Its title therefore gives a stronger impression than the primary result supports. [Yasuda trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7330467/), [registration dates](https://center6.umin.ac.jp/cgi-open-bin/ctr_e/ctr_view.cgi?recptno=R000042763)

## What happens when the experiment uses an entire diet?

A newer controlled-feeding trial provided all meals to 40 young adults for nine days. Protein was about 1.1–1.2 g/kg/day, arranged as three dinner-heavy meals or five balanced occasions, within omnivorous or vegan diets. It found no effect of distribution or diet source on integrated muscle protein synthesis during resistance training. This measurement followed several days of ordinary activity, which makes it more relevant to daily eating than one isolated shake. Nevertheless, it measured synthesis rather than months of strength or growth, and the variability leaves smaller effects uncertain. It cannot establish that this lower protein intake maximizes hypertrophy. Beef Checkoff funded the study. [Askow trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40197715/), [original accepted manuscript](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390596520_Impact_of_Vegan_Diets_on_Resistance_Exercise-Mediated_Myofibrillar_Protein_Synthesis_in_Healthy_Young_Males_and_Females_A_Randomized_Controlled_Trial)

None of these schedules tested continuously sipping diluted yogurt. A routine that is enjoyable and manageable can be retained without claiming that tiny sips are uniquely optimal. An occasional recognizable eating occasion may help appetite and planning; the evidence reviewed here does not establish a minimum protein quota for each sip or snack.

## Faster absorption and food quality answer different questions

In a 20-man experiment, cheese and milk concentrate each supplied 30 g protein after exercise. Milk produced a larger amino-acid peak, but four-hour muscle protein synthesis did not differ. The cheese supplied more fat and energy, so this was not a calorie-matched test of processing alone. It supports caution about judging a dairy food by absorption speed; it does not establish that cheese is superior to yogurt or that either creates identical long-term gains. The Dutch Dairy Association funded it, and two authors disclosed dairy-company relationships. [Cheese versus concentrate](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8971000/)

A 2026 study compared protein-matched egg whites, whole eggs, salmon, pork, lentils and mycoprotein after exercise. It recruited 72 participants; 65 contributed study data, with 58 in the final muscle-synthesis analysis. Despite different amino-acid responses, it detected no between-food difference in five-hour muscle synthesis, its primary outcome; a secondary estimate of whole-body protein balance favored egg whites. Neither result establishes future hypertrophy. The foods were matched for protein, not all other nutrients. Missing biopsy data, an interim sample-size revision and small groups limit certainty. Marlow Foods sponsored the project and participated in discussions; two authors were company employees when it was designed. [Whole-food comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13084606/)

Food differences can still matter. Another trial found greater five-hour muscle synthesis after lower-fat than higher-fat ground pork at the same 20 g protein. It involved 16 adults, unequal calories and an incomplete crossover design in which each participant received at most two of three conditions; the National Pork Board funded it. That result concerns those particular pork meals. It does not show that chicken skin, avocado or chimichurri harms training, or that removing all meal fat improves growth. [Ground-pork comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12799377/)

For the present shopping routine, enjoyable yogurt, chicken and fish can remain ordinary food choices. Their exact digestion rates need not dictate a new product. If meal size or timing becomes a problem, first record approximate eating times, hunger, digestion and workout timing for several days. That would identify a practical problem to solve with the foods already being bought, rather than assume the current pattern needs correction.
