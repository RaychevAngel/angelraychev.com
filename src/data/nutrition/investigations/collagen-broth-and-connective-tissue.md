## The question behind the bottle

I prefer drinking broth to taking collagen powder. I have also reported increasing training and front-of-shoulder discomfort that became progressively more uncomfortable over about a week. Those are separate facts: a food preference does not identify the painful tissue or establish that collagen, glycine or a vitamin is missing.

Nutrition can support recovery, but a progressively worsening shoulder deserves assessment and adjustment of the movements that aggravate it. Sudden severe pain, inability to move the arm, marked swelling, persistent numbness or pain after a substantial injury need prompt medical attention. The food log cannot distinguish tendon pain from other shoulder problems. [NHS shoulder guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/shoulder-pain/).

## What the famous gelatin experiment showed

Shaw and colleagues studied **eight healthy men** in a crossover experiment. They drank placebo, **5 g or 15 g gelatin**, each with **48 mg vitamin C**, one hour before six minutes of rope-skipping. Supplementation and activity continued three times daily over three days. The 15 g condition increased circulating collagen-related amino acids and roughly doubled the measured PINP response relative to the other conditions. Serum from the participants also affected collagen production in engineered ligament tissue. [Shaw et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5183725/).

That is a useful proof of concept, with two important boundaries. **Blood PINP is largely a bone-formation marker, not a direct measurement of a shoulder tendon healing.** The authors explicitly discuss this. Engineered ligament tissue exposed to serum is a laboratory model. The study did not measure fewer injuries, quicker return to sport or improvement in shoulder pain. It also did not isolate vitamin C from gelatin: vitamin C was present in every drink.

## The evidence did not stop there

Different measurements give different answers. In **45 young recreational athletes**, 30 g collagen after resistance exercise increased circulating glycine and proline, but did **not significantly increase muscle connective protein synthesis** over the five-hour recovery period compared with placebo. Whey significantly increased myofibrillar synthesis relative to placebo; collagen did not, but the direct whey–collagen comparison was also nonsignificant. These were muscle biopsies, a more direct tissue measurement than a circulating marker, but still not an injured tendon or a long-term healing outcome. [Aussieker et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10487367/).

A **2025 trial in another 45 young adults** compared 30 g hydrolyzed collagen, a matching mixture of its free amino acids and placebo after unilateral resistance exercise. Neither supplement significantly increased muscle connective or myofibrillar synthesis in rested or exercised muscle over six hours. More amino acids in the blood therefore did not automatically mean more synthesis in that tissue. [Aussieker et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40523226/).

Longer training trials provide some positive signals. In **50 healthy men**, 5 g of specific collagen peptides daily during 14 weeks of resistance training produced greater patellar-tendon cross-sectional area at some measured locations. Tendon stiffness, muscle size and knee-extension strength improved with training without significant between-group differences. In **20 middle-aged men**, a different 12-week protocol using **30 g collagen plus 50 mg vitamin C after training** improved tendon size and mechanical properties more than placebo plus vitamin C; muscle strength improvements did not significantly differ between groups. These findings concern tendon adaptation, not proof of fewer ruptures or faster recovery from an existing shoulder injury. [Jerger et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37424319/), [Nulty et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40100255/).

There is also a relevant longer-term null result. In **39 healthy young men**, 15 g collagen daily during 15 weeks of lower-body resistance training did **not** improve tendon size or mechanical adaptation more than placebo. Tendon stiffness rose in both groups, about **17% with collagen and 21% with placebo**, without a significant treatment difference. Collagen therefore has not consistently added benefit even for these structural outcomes. [Balshaw et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37436929/).

A **June 2026 report in 29 young men** adds a longer-term muscle-tissue finding. After 12 weeks of resistance training, 15 g daily of specific collagen peptides produced a greater increase in the type I collagen staining signal: **29.8% versus 9.9%**. Type III and IV changes did not significantly differ between groups. This measured immunofluorescence intensity in muscle sections, rather than synthesis rate or injury recovery. Its placebo contained no protein or calories, so it cannot establish whether collagen outperforms another added protein. The manufacturer partly funded the study, and one author disclosed collagen-related patents. It is a useful tissue finding alongside the acute synthesis nulls above; the methods and timescales differ. [Jerger et al., 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.1839695/full).

The biopsy paper is a later report from an existing trial cohort, rather than independent replication of its earlier molecular report. Its registry labels registration retrospective and lists long-term histology as secondary; the primary endpoint concerns mTOR activation. This limits claims about prospective confirmation of the selected stain finding. [Original trial registry](https://drks.de/search/en/trial/DRKS00027112).

## Pain and function are a harder test

A pilot in **20 patients with chronic mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy** used a specific commercial collagen preparation, **2.5 g twice daily**, alongside a structured calf-strengthening and return-to-running program. In the first three months, the collagen-first group improved its VISA-A pain/function score by **12.6 points**, versus **5.3 points** in the placebo-first group. Both improved, then treatments crossed over. [Praet et al., 2019](https://researchsystem.canberra.edu.au/ws/files/25122853/Praet2019.pdf).

This is a clinical signal worth investigating, but it is a small, manufacturer-funded pilot with exercise treatment in both groups and an awkward crossover design for an improving chronic condition. Its authors say that firm conclusions about added benefit require a larger trial. A product-specific result in Achilles tendinopathy cannot be transferred directly to ordinary broth or undiagnosed shoulder pain.

Shoulder-specific research also needs careful interpretation. A study in **90 patients after surgical repair of a large rotator-cuff tear** reported less pain and better MRI repair integrity with an oral product combining collagen, arginine, MSM and bromelain, but no better objective functional outcome. It cannot isolate collagen, and a postsurgical population differs greatly from my reported discomfort. Shoulder-bursa collagen injections test a different intervention again; they are not evidence for drinking broth. [Gumina et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23043451/), [Godek et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36536333/).

## Why broth is not a known collagen dose

An analysis of commercial, homemade and café bone broths found substantial differences in collagen-related amino acids. Standardized broths generally supplied less of the relevant amino acids than the **20 g collagen supplement comparator**. Some café preparations were richer, but the variability makes “one bottle” an unreliable way to reproduce an experiment. This was a composition study, not evidence that broth improved injuries. [Alcock et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29893587/).

A protein label reports **total protein**, not grams of collagen, glycine or a studied peptide preparation. Without a verified assay, I should log those specific amounts as unknown. Broth can still be an enjoyable food. Compare the amount I will actually drink, protein and sodium per entire package, ingredients and price. A higher price or “bone” on the label does not establish a therapeutic dose, and drinking more solely to reach an assumed dose can add considerable sodium or displace meals.

For the current daily-shopping setup, an unopened shelf-stable single portion is practical if its label permits room-temperature storage. Once opened, follow its refrigeration directions. A perishable bottle carried around and sipped all day needs temperature control; the usual limit outside refrigeration is **two hours, or one hour above 90°F/32°C**. [FDA storage guidance](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/are-you-storing-food-safely).

## Glycine, vitamin C and the rest of the diet

Glycine is abundant in collagen, but isolated glycine is not proven shoulder-injury treatment. A frequently cited Achilles experiment used a **5% glycine diet in rats**. That establishes an animal finding, not a human dose or outcome. A small human sleep-restriction crossover study analyzed only **seven participants** taking 3 g glycine before bed; it found some next-day fatigue and vigilance improvements, not tendon healing. [Vieira et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25156668/), [Bannai et al., 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3328957/).

The latest food estimate already contains substantial vitamin C from the pepper and fruit. The gelatin study used tens of milligrams, so its design does not justify adding gram-dose vitamin C to this diet. A collagen product also has a different amino-acid composition from dairy, fish or chicken; its muscle-building value needs its own evidence rather than being inferred from total protein grams.

The practical choice is to keep broth optional because I enjoy it, verify its package rather than assigning a collagen dose, and build recovery around enough food, ordinary protein, sleep and appropriate loading. A collagen experiment would be an adjunct with a defined goal and outcome; it would not make the shoulder diagnosis unnecessary. The study doses here describe research protocols, not a personalized prescription.
