## Start with the actual problem

My latest record includes four bananas, an orange and raspberries. I enjoyed fruit; the oversized meal on an earlier day became too much to finish. The practical question is how to keep food enjoyable and manageable. It is not a measured problem with blood glucose or liver health.

The [sugar guide](/nutrition/guide/sugars/) explains the different sugar categories and the existing apple-preload evidence. [Fruit substitutions](/nutrition/research/fruit-substitutions/) offers optional variety. Here, the question is what changes when fruit becomes a drink.

## Fullness does not always predict the next meal

In a controlled crossover, **47 adults completed** tests of fruit salad, a commercial smoothie and other drinks after an overnight fast. Non-water portions provided about **136 kcal**; salad and smoothie contained similar fiber. Salad increased fullness more, yet next-meal energy compensation did not differ significantly between energy-containing options. Pairwise fullness comparisons were not adjusted for multiple testing. Compensation was lower with a two-hour delay than an immediate meal. Eating speed varied naturally. Innocent funded the experiment and was consulted on design. Stronger fullness did not necessarily mean less food eaten; the null comparison does not establish equal long-term effects. [Rogers and Shahrokni, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5946216/)

## A meal changes the glucose comparison

A **2025 randomized crossover** compared orange pieces, 250 mL orange juice and a sugar-matched drink alongside a carbohydrate-rich breakfast. In **15 older adults with treated type 2 diabetes**, integrated glucose and insulin responses did not differ significantly; insulin analysis included 13 people. The fruit's actual sugar content varied across servings. A small acute null result does not establish equivalence, long-term safety or the response of a healthy young adult. The Fruit Juice Science Centre funded it; a coauthor disclosed juice-industry consultancy. [Verboven and colleagues, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41387-025-00385-8)

## A lower peak is a narrower result

A **2026 crossover** analyzed 25 young men drinking 300 mL orange juice or drinks with the same sugar composition and less fruit material after fasting. Juice lowered the glucose peak versus the sugar-only drink, but **two-hour integrated glucose did not differ significantly among the three sugar-matched drinks**. The current registry lists integrated glucose as primary and peak as secondary; historical versions were not inspected here. Energy and other constituents differed. There was no whole-orange arm. Funding came partly from the Fruit Juice Science Centre, with disclosed industry consultancy. This supports a limited peak result, not a personalized juice prescription. [García-Conesa and colleagues, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1039/D5FO04536C)

## Longer studies still need a fair comparator

In an eight-week crossover, **43 adults were recruited and 40 completed** two-apples-per-day and apple-squash periods; cholesterol analysis included 38. Total cholesterol, the paper's primary outcome, was lower after apples. These were adults with mildly raised cholesterol. Although the abstract calls the control energy-matched, measured portions provided **221 versus 167 kcal**, with substantially different fiber and polyphenols. The squash also contained added sugar. This compares dietary packages, rather than isolating physical form or testing ordinary 100% juice. Apples were supplied by a grower cooperative. Marker changes are not measured prevention of heart attacks. [Koutsos and colleagues, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6997084/)

## What this changes for me

I can keep enjoyable fruit, optional variety and portions that fit appetite. This review adopts no new purchase, fruit restriction or changed intake.
