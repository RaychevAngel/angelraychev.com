One attractive frontier is measuring a restorative process more directly than a sleep-stage label. Brain-fluid transport is a candidate. The glymphatic hypothesis describes exchange between cerebrospinal fluid, the fluid surrounding the brain and spinal cord, and interstitial fluid within brain tissue. The proposed exchange helps move soluble material through and out of the brain.

This makes a precise question worth pursuing: can we measure a process that matters for brain health, improve it, and demonstrate a useful result? The evidence provides promising parts of that chain, with important gaps between them.

## First define the quantity

Fluid oscillation, movement of a tracer, removal of a particular protein and long-term preservation of cognition are different endpoints. Fluid can move back and forth without a proportional net export of material. A tracer’s concentration reflects arrival, departure and redistribution. A protein concentration also depends on production, release and degradation.

For this project, a measurement should identify which part of that chain it observes. A proposed “brain-clearance score” would need a validation argument, a stable measurement protocol and a defined relationship to an outcome we care about.

## Human sleep changes brain-fluid dynamics

Fultz and colleagues combined EEG with fast functional MRI in 13 people. During NREM sleep, slow electrical activity was coupled to changes in blood-related signals and waves of cerebrospinal fluid inflow. This demonstrated coordinated human physiology. It did not measure removal of amyloid, tau or other solutes, or test future dementia risk. Several investigators disclosed a patent coversheet. [Fultz et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax5440).

A 2026 study used EEG and ultrafast MRI in 22 healthy volunteers to estimate the velocity and direction of physiological brain pulsations. Respiratory and vasomotor components increased in particular regions during NREM sleep, while cardiac-related velocity decreased. The analysis was calibrated against a flow phantom. Its endpoint was pulsation dynamics, rather than the amount of a metabolite removed. [Elabasy et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/advs.202503745).

These studies make breathing, vascular rhythms and neural activity interesting mechanisms. They also show why a single “lower is better” target is inadequate: different components change in different directions. A respiratory-rate estimate from a ring does not establish those intracranial dynamics.

## A human tracer experiment

Eide and colleagues compared seven sleep-deprived participants with 17 matched controls allowed to sleep, following a tracer injected into the cerebrospinal fluid with repeated MRI. Clearance was slower after deprivation, and the difference persisted after the subsequent recovery opportunity within the study window. Participants were patients undergoing work-up for suspected CSF disorders; allocation was not randomized. This was a small, clinically selected population using an invasive, off-label tracer procedure. [Eide et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awaa443).

The study supports concern about acute deprivation and molecular transport. It does not determine an optimum deep-sleep duration in a healthy young adult, or establish that recovery sleep can never compensate for lost sleep. Those broader propositions exceed its design and follow-up.

## Mouse experiments reveal a mechanism and a dispute

Hauglund and colleagues measured norepinephrine, vascular signals and fluid transport in mice. They linked slow norepinephrine oscillations during NREM sleep to vasomotion and glymphatic transport; experimental manipulations helped test the mechanism. Zolpidem suppressed aspects of the rhythm and flow. Funding included public agencies and research foundations; an author disclosed consultancy for unrelated work. [Hauglund et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2024.11.027).

The relevant insight is that sleep’s internal organization can matter beyond time assigned to NREM. The findings do not establish a human medication’s effect on dementia or a target that a consumer ring can measure.

There is also a substantive methodological disagreement. Miao and colleagues inferred reduced clearance during sleep and anesthesia from fluorescent-dye experiments in male mice. Their tracer was placed in brain tissue, and the clearance inference relied on its distribution and a mathematical model. The paper received public and charitable support and declared no competing interests. [Miao et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-024-01638-y).

A published critique challenged its methods and assumptions, and the authors defended them in reply. A separate correction amended a source-data column. Those records belong beside the original claim. The disagreement concerns how transport and clearance are inferred; treating either headline as the final answer would conceal the question that needs resolving. [Plá et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-025-01897-3), [Franks and Wisden’s reply](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-025-01898-2), [source-data correction](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-024-01698-0).

## Emerging human devices: closer to the process, still a proxy

In 2025, Dagum and colleagues described an investigational in-ear device combining EEG with electrical-impedance measurements intended to reflect changes in brain parenchymal resistance. Two clinical studies compared sleep with deprivation; validation included contrast-enhanced MRI after intravenous injection. The reference measure itself required a model of contrast movement. Applied Cognition funded the study, and several authors disclosed financial or stock-option interests. [Device study](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41551-025-01394-9).

A 2026 paper analyzed plasma amyloid and tau alongside physiological recordings from those same cohorts. The abstract reports 39 participants; the results describe 38 in the current analysis. The raw overnight biomarker changes did not significantly differ between sleep and deprivation. The clearance interpretation emerged from multivariate models linking physiology, release and transport. This is more specific than a direct experimental demonstration that sleep increased every measured blood biomarker. Applied Cognition funded this work too, with financial and stock-option disclosures. [Dagum et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-68374-8).

These publications develop a possible measurement approach; they do not provide independent replications in wholly new samples. Clinical benefit, dementia prevention and a personalized optimum remain additional questions.

## What would earn a place among my targets

The next step is a measurement and intervention question, rather than choosing a desired clearance number. I would require:

- **A defined observable:** which fluid, solute, region and time window does the measurement concern?
- **Repeatability and validation:** how much changes with fitting, placement, ordinary physiology or measurement error?
- **An intervention test:** can a feasible change improve the relevant process under controlled comparison?
- **A functional connection:** does that improvement accompany better memory, attention or another meaningful endpoint?
- **An applicable population:** does the evidence extend to a healthy young person with my circumstances?

Better sleep remains worth pursuing on the [human performance evidence](/sleep/research/why-sleep-matters/). Brain-clearance research expands what we can investigate. Until those measurement questions are answered, neither wearable deep-sleep minutes nor a blood biomarker should be translated into a personal brain-cleaning achievement.
