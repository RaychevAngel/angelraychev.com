**A convincing result is a useful effect that survives repetition under described conditions.** The point of the record is to make that achievement visible: what was changed, why, what happened and what the evidence establishes.

The [target specification](/sleep/targets/) distinguishes a device-score benchmark from a physiological optimum. The [strategy queue](/sleep/research/improvement-strategies/) identifies promising interventions. This page describes how to connect them without turning a successful night into a claim it cannot support.

No personal sleep intervention has yet been completed or validated here. The first entry is a baseline observation, not a before-and-after experiment.

## Start with an answerable question

“Improve sleep” is an ambition. “Does moving my usual last caffeine exposure earlier increase obtained sleep without reducing useful daytime performance?” is a testable question.

The specification should contain:

| Item | What must be explicit |
|---|---|
| Objective | The outcome worth improving |
| Change | One defined difference, including dose, duration or timing |
| Primary measure | The quantity used to judge the result |
| Supporting measures | A small set that helps interpret benefit and tradeoffs |
| Worthwhile effect | An improvement large enough to justify the cost |
| Comparison | What the changed condition is compared with |
| Eligibility | Which nights belong, how missingness and exceptions are handled |
| Duration and carryover | How long to test and whether yesterday affects today |
| Decision | Retain, reject, repeat or extend under a stated rule |

A proposed twenty-to-thirty-minute duration gain could be a practical decision threshold. It is not a universal clinical cutoff. Other questions need other thresholds. A score gain can count as a score gain while physiological and health claims remain unresolved.

## Establish ordinary variation

An initial **seven-to-fourteen-night baseline** is a practical proposal, not a scientifically sufficient sample for every question. It can reveal missing data, ordinary variability and recording problems before we try to detect an effect.

Retain device identity, known firmware or algorithm changes, timezone and episode boundaries. Record actual caffeine and alcohol consumption, naps, exercise, illness/travel and any relevant environment. Unknown context remains unknown. Planned changes must remain separate from completed actions.

A short morning note adds observations the sensors cannot supply: perceived restfulness, unusual awakenings and daytime sleepiness. Feeling well matters, but subjective adaptation can miss accumulating performance impairment during restriction. That is a reason to include an independent functional measure. [Laboratory restriction experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/)

## Choose a design suited to the intervention

**Repeated, randomized or counterbalanced nights** can suit a reversible environmental change. They reduce the chance that an ordinary weekly pattern or a gradually improving training state explains the result. They do not magically remove expectation effects or poor adherence.

**Longer blocks** suit interventions whose effects accumulate or shift phase: sleep opportunity, regularity and coherent light patterns. Alternating them nightly can erase the intended contrast or create carryover. A repeated A/B/A/B pattern can help distinguish an intervention effect from a simple time trend, where returning to the comparison is sensible.

The appropriate stabilization or washout period depends on the intervention. We should specify it from the underlying research, not use one number for caffeine, phase shifting and a room adjustment. A sudden caffeine reduction may introduce withdrawal; changing training timing can also change available recovery and meals.

Useful comparisons do not require deliberately making sleep poor. They can compare usual conditions with an improvement or use naturally occurring exposures, with their causal limits stated.

## Measure function consistently

Vigilance is a promising independent outcome because it concerns performance while awake. A validated psychomotor vigilance task is a candidate; a custom reaction-time game should not silently inherit its validity.

A three-minute PVT has demonstrated predictive value in a laboratory luggage-screening experiment. A later 83-person sleep-loss/recovery study found that its responses were not interchangeable with the ten-minute version. A shorter test reduces burden, but protocol and sensitivity remain part of the choice. We should select a version and validate its usefulness for this project, rather than change task length while interpreting the results as one continuous series. [Brief-test study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21912278/), [convergence study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35242006/)

Before collecting a baseline, settle the task, device, duration, testing time relative to waking and stimulant condition. Allow familiarization. Preserve lapses and the response distribution when the protocol calls for them rather than keeping only the fastest response. Motivation, hardware latency and practice can move a score without a sleep change.

Training measurements need the same discipline. A harder workout, accumulated fatigue or an easier training block can explain a change. “Recovery” should refer to a specified outcome, such as repeatable performance or reported soreness, rather than whatever number happens to improve. The [outcome investigation](/sleep/research/why-sleep-matters/) supplies the rationale for choosing measures.

## Keep the whole eligible result

Publish adherence, the planned comparison, all eligible nights and exclusions. An illness or sensor failure can be flagged; it should not disappear merely because it spoils the mean. Decide exclusion rules before inspecting the desired effect whenever possible.

Report effect size and spread. Nights are correlated, so ten sequential nights are not ten fully independent participants. A before/after mean alone hides variation, missingness and secular change. Appropriate uncertainty estimates should respect the design and the available number of blocks.

Large stage changes on a single night require particular caution: the observed value contains biological variation and device estimation error. [Measurement and validation](/sleep/research/measurement-and-validation/) explains why an apparently precise estimate can still be uncertain.

## What counts as progress

| Result | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Higher Oura grade | Improvement under a particular scoring algorithm | A quantified health or longevity benefit |
| More estimated sleep | A change in the device's duration estimate | Accurate staging or improved next-day function |
| Replicated functional improvement | Better measured performance under the tested conditions | A universal optimum or benefit in every training phase |
| Two devices agree | Agreement between those instruments | Agreement with a reference standard |
| A consistent association | A useful candidate explanation | An isolated causal effect |
| No clear effect | The tested design did not resolve a useful effect | Proof that the intervention never works |

Once a benefit is repeatable, test whether it transfers to another block, heavier training or travel. Once a milestone is achieved, identify the next useful question. Expansion can mean better validation, a stronger outcome or a more resilient result; it does not have to mean buying another sensor.

## Realistic timescales

Within a few hours we can establish definitions, source-linked hypotheses and the first record. Over one or two weeks we can characterize ordinary variation and sometimes see a conspicuous acute response. Over several weeks, a focused repeated comparison can estimate a practical personal effect. Small changes, carryover and changing training load may require longer.

A few weeks of data cannot establish disease prevention, lifespan extension or an effect of every sleep stage. Those are different research questions. The ambition remains large; the next experiment should be narrow enough to produce a useful answer.
