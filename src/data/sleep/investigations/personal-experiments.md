**A convincing result is a useful effect that survives repetition under described conditions.** This page connects the [targets](/sleep/targets/) and [strategy queue](/sleep/research/improvement-strategies/) to a decision: what to change, how to test it and whether to keep it. The first entry is a baseline observation; no personal intervention has yet been validated.

## Start with an answerable question

“Improve sleep” is an ambition. “Does moving my usual last caffeine exposure earlier increase obtained sleep without reducing useful daytime performance?” is a testable question.

The specification should contain:

| Item | What must be explicit |
|---|---|
| Objective | The outcome worth improving |
| Change | One defined difference, including dose, duration or timing |
| Primary measure | The quantity used to judge the result |
| Supporting measures | A small set that helps interpret benefit and tradeoffs |
| Worthwhile effect | An improvement large enough to justify the cost |
| Comparison | What the changed condition is compared with |
| Eligibility | Which nights belong, how missingness and exceptions are handled |
| Duration and carryover | How long to test and whether yesterday affects today |
| Decision | Retain, reject, repeat or extend under a stated rule |

A proposed twenty-to-thirty-minute duration gain could be a practical decision threshold, distinct from a clinical cutoff. Other questions need other thresholds.

## Establish ordinary variation

An initial **seven-to-fourteen-night baseline** can reveal ordinary variation and recording problems. It is a practical proposal, not sufficient evidence for every question. Retain device identity, algorithm changes, timezone and episode boundaries; separate planned changes from completed actions.

Subjective restfulness adds information, but can miss accumulating performance impairment during restriction. Include a functional measure alongside the sensor and morning note. [Laboratory restriction experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/)

## The smallest useful context record

Record what changes interpretation, while keeping the recurring burden visible.

| Context | Minimum useful description | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Opportunity | Intended sleep opportunity and whether it was curtailed | Separates inability to sleep from insufficient time allocated |
| Stimulants and alcohol | None, unknown, or actual amount and timing | Makes an exposure hypothesis eligible for investigation |
| Naps and training | Nap opportunity/estimated sleep; session type, strain and end time | Preserves total sleep and training recovery context |
| Morning | Restfulness, unusual awakenings and sleepiness under a consistent scale | Adds observations the device cannot provide |
| Exceptions | Illness, travel, medication changes or notable disturbance | Explains potentially important differences without deleting the night |

Add room observations for an environment experiment or intake/timing for a meal question. Expand the record when a question requires it. Unknown must remain distinct from zero exposure.

## Choose a design suited to the intervention

Single-person crossover guidance emphasizes explicit periods, sequence, outcomes, carryover and within-person correlation. It suits reversible interventions whose effects can be separated in time. [CENT guidance](https://www.bmj.com/content/350/bmj.h1793)

**Randomized or counterbalanced nights** can suit a reversible room adjustment, reducing confounding by weekday patterns or a gradually changing training state. Expectation and adherence still matter.

**Longer repeated blocks** suit accumulating or phase-shifting effects: sleep opportunity, regularity and coherent light patterns. Nightly alternation can create carryover or erase the contrast. Repeating blocks helps assess time trends when returning to the comparison is sensible.

Specify stabilization and washout from the intervention's evidence. They differ for caffeine, phase shifting and a room adjustment. Compare usual conditions with an improvement or use naturally occurring exposures; deliberate sleep loss is unnecessary.

## A caffeine timing comparison

After actual use is recorded, a candidate comparison is usual versus earlier timing **at the same dose**. Keep other caffeine exposures and intended sleep opportunity comparable; record timing, training and adherence. Define the interval from planned opportunity and retain actual onset as an outcome. A caffeine-induced delay could otherwise make the dose look farther from sleep. No additional late dose is needed.

Gardiner's acute trial separated conditions by 48 hours and maintained habitual morning caffeine. This is protocol context, not a universal personal washout. [Trial protocol](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/48/4/zsae230/7815486)

Prior-night debt can persist after the stimulant's immediate effect diminishes. Changing total intake adds adaptation and withdrawal. For a daily habitual-pattern comparison, use stabilized repeated blocks and prespecify how transition nights are retained or flagged.

Test next-day performance at a consistent relationship to morning caffeine. Mixing caffeinated and uncaffeinated tests would confound direct stimulation with the preceding night's effect. Retain the daytime and training tradeoff.

## Measure function consistently

A validated psychomotor vigilance task measures waking performance. A custom reaction-time game needs its own validation.

A three-minute PVT predicted performance in a laboratory luggage-screening experiment. A later 83-person study found inadequate interchangeability with the ten-minute version during sleep loss/recovery. A shorter test reduces burden but changes sensitivity. Choose one protocol before baseline collection. [Brief-test study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21912278/), [convergence study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35242006/)

Keep task, device, duration and stimulant condition consistent. Allow familiarization and retain lapses and response distributions. Motivation, latency and practice can move results independently of sleep.

Choose testing time from the question. A duration comparison can match time since waking. A phase experiment targeting performance at a real clock-time demand should test that window and record the changed time since waking. That change is part of the intended practical effect; holding it constant would answer a different question.

Recording the morning rating before viewing the grade is a useful expectation control. In a randomized 63-person insomnia experiment, false positive/negative sleep feedback changed symptom appraisal but not vigilance performance. Its clinical population does not establish the size of this effect here. NHS/Wellcome/Sackler funding was reported; complete conflict review remains unverified. [Gavriloff et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29989248/)

Training comparisons need a specified outcome and comparable load. Accumulated fatigue or an easier block can explain change; “recovery” should refer to repeatable performance or another named outcome. [Rationale for outcome selection](/sleep/research/why-sleep-matters/).

## Keep the whole eligible result

Publish adherence, all eligible nights and prespecified exclusions. Flag illness or sensor failure without removing inconvenient results. Report the effect and its spread; sequential nights are correlated, and a before/after mean can hide missingness and time trends.

Consider a **hypothetical** nightly standard deviation of 45 minutes. Fourteen independent nights per condition give a mean-difference standard error near 17 minutes: `45 × √(2/14)`. An approximate 95% interval extends roughly 35 minutes either way. These are invented inputs, not measured variability or a sample-size prescription. Carryover and correlation can widen uncertainty, so a small worthwhile effect may need more data.

Biological variation and device error also affect apparent stage changes. [Measurement and validation](/sleep/research/measurement-and-validation/) explains the relevant limits.

## A milestone and a decision

Keep three decisions separate: **was the benchmark achieved, should the intervention be retained, and which benefit was established?** The [targets](/sleep/targets/) define award rules; the planned experiment defines the retain/reject rule.

A score milestone can be achieved while the change is rejected because functioning reproducibly worsened. A functional gain can justify retaining a change even when the score stays flat. If function is unmeasured, report it as unknown. An unresolved comparison remains unresolved rather than proving universal ineffectiveness.

After a repeatable benefit, assess transfer to heavier training or travel. Expansion can strengthen the outcome or validation as well as add instrumentation.

## Realistic timescales

Definitions and hypotheses can be established within hours. One or two weeks can characterize ordinary variation; several weeks can support a focused repeated comparison. Small effects, carryover and changing training load may require longer. Personal sleep or function gains are attainable much sooner than demonstrated disease prevention or lifespan extension.
