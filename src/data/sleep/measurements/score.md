## What it represents

Oura's Sleep Score is a composite of total sleep, efficiency, restfulness, REM, deep sleep, latency and timing. Contributors and the composite are graded on a 0–100 scale; 85–100 is the provider's Optimal category. They are not percentages of a physiological process. [Oura score](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/en-us/articles/360025445574-Sleep-Score), [contributors](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/en-us/articles/360057792293-Sleep-Contributors).

The same night can receive different grades from different devices because both the underlying estimates and the scoring rules differ. Oura gave the [first recorded night](/sleep/record/#night-2026-10-13) 75; COROS gave its selected episode 93. An 18-point difference is not an 18% difference in actual restoration.

## The proposed target

The [target specification](/sleep/targets/#score-milestones) proposes a 14-day average ≥85, then a 28-day average ≥90 with a consistency and coverage requirement. These are explicit project challenges. Their windows and thresholds are choices; no trial establishes that moving from 89 to 90 causes a discrete health improvement.

A score increase can be pursued and celebrated as a score increase. To establish a broader benefit, examine what changed and whether an appropriate outcome changed as well. Examples include obtained sleep, unwanted disruption, a validated vigilance task and reported functioning. A high score with worse daytime performance is a disagreement to investigate, not a reason to erase the latter observation.

## What the first two nights suggest

Oura's timing contributor was 12, total sleep 71, REM 81, restfulness 93, deep sleep 95 and efficiency 98; latency was 78. These locate weaker grading dimensions. They do not tell us which intervention will improve a meaningful outcome.

Timing needs two separate investigations: what Oura penalizes and whether the current sleep episode is suitably aligned with internal phase and daily demands. Deep sleep already received a high contributor score. There is no basis in this night alone for treating extra estimated deep minutes as the dominant opportunity.

The second night's composite rose to 84. Total-sleep grading rose from 71 to 91 and timing from 12 to 29, while restfulness fell from 93 to 79 and efficiency grading from 98 to 90. These component changes describe a higher composite alongside worse grading of continuity. The account's cold awakenings remain relevant even though COROS awarded 95. [Second record](/sleep/record/#night-2026-10-14).

This is why the project retains the score, its components and personal experience. The next decision concerns a [comfortable routine and unwanted waking](/sleep/research/personal-experiments/#the-current-routine-proposal), with score improvement as a supporting result. A causal or sustained benefit has not yet been demonstrated.

## Measurement and interpretation

The third night reversed part of the tradeoff: Oura's score fell to 71 while restfulness rose to 92 and timing to 78. Total-sleep grading was 51 and REM 30; COROS scored 80. The main episode was shorter, although I felt much better on waking. A score decline does not erase that positive experience, and a positive experience does not establish sufficient duration. [October 15 record](/sleep/record/#night-2026-10-15).

On [16 October](/sleep/record/#night-2026-10-16), Oura returned 90 after synchronization, with 8h37m30s estimated sleep. Total-sleep grading rose to 93 and REM to 97; efficiency and restfulness were 98 and 93. Not every contributor improved: latency grading was 67 and timing 72. COROS was still pending. This is one encouraging grade, not achievement of either sustained milestone. The later sleepiness now reported for October 15 also keeps the full following day relevant to interpreting the next score.

Sleep staging validation does not validate every composite-score threshold. A score is downstream of several estimates and an algorithmic judgment. Firmware or scoring changes can alter it without a lifestyle change. Keep hardware and algorithm information when exposed and mark known changes.

Use one named primary score series. Keep COROS as its own series and retain disagreement. Do not select the device with the better grade on each date, average the two scores into a purported biological measure, or merge Sleep Score with Readiness.

The useful record contains the score, all contributors, coverage, context and functional observations. It preserves difficult nights and missingness, so a sustained milestone means what the [assessment rule](/sleep/targets/#score-milestones) says it means.
